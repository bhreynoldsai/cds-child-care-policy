## 3.3 Arizona — 30 centers

> **Snapshot.** A single-metro concentration operating in a low-regulation, low-subsidy, divided-government state. Two facts dominate: the state child care assistance **waitlist has grown 575 percent in twenty-four months** and continues to grow faster than appropriations can release children from it; and **First Things First, the voter-created tobacco-tax early childhood agency, is structurally decaying** as smokers convert to vaping products the 2006 ballot measure does not tax. A constitutionally indexed minimum wage compounds both.

### Licensing and regulation

Centers are licensed by the Arizona Department of Health Services, Bureau of Child Care Licensing, under A.A.C. Title 9, Chapter 5. **The operative version is Supplement 25-2, effective August 3, 2025.**

**A document trap worth flagging to compliance staff:** the PDF that ADHS still serves on its own website is the superseded **September 30, 2010** version and still uses the retired job title "teacher-caregiver." Cornell's mirror of R9-5-404 is likewise keyed to the 2010 amendment. Compliance staff should work from the Secretary of State's Supplement 25-2 text.

The August 2025 rule revision changed:

| Area | Change |
|---|---|
| Job titles | "Teacher-Caregiver" → **"Child Educator"**; "Assistant Teacher-Caregiver" → "Assistant Child Educator" |
| **Annual training** | Increased to **24 hours per year — compliance deadline August 2026** (up from 18) |
| New-staff orientation | Expanded to cover infant tummy time, SIDS prevention, shaken baby syndrome |
| Closure reporting | Notify ADHS **within 24 hours** of any closure, planned or unplanned |
| TB screening | Mandatory Mantoux testing replaced by a self-screening form |
| Infant sleep | Cribs: fitted sheets only; blankets prohibited |
| Discipline | Section reframed as "Positive Discipline and Guidance" |
| Suspension/expulsion | Programs must adopt **written suspension and expulsion policies** with prevention strategies |
| Attendance | "Signature or other unique identifier" accepted — enables digital check-in |
| Plans | Site and floor plans no longer require an architect's seal |

**The 24-hour annual training compliance deadline falls this month.** Auditing training records across all thirty sites is the most probable near-term citation avoidance available in the Arizona portfolio.

Ratios under R9-5-404 are among the most permissive in the country — infants 1:5, one-year-olds 1:6, two-year-olds 1:8, three-year-olds **1:13**, four-year-olds **1:15**, five-year-olds and school-age 1:20. **Arizona imposes no hard maximum group sizes;** instead the licensee must maintain the required ratio for each group **based on the age of the youngest child in the group**. That mixed-age rule is the operationally binding constraint.

*Caveat:* the R9-5-404 text could not be read out of the Supplement 25-2 PDF. Ratios are very likely unchanged — the 2025 rulemaking summaries describe no ratio change — but this should be confirmed from pages 18 to 22 of the Secretary of State PDF before staffing models rely on it.

Staff qualification floors are likewise permissive relative to peer states: **Child Educators must be 18 with six months' experience and a high school diploma; Assistant Child Educators may be 16** with current high school enrollment, a diploma or twelve months' prior experience.

**Background checks are portable and that is a genuine hiring-velocity advantage** — Arizona DPS Fingerprint Clearance Cards are valid six years and move with the employee across employers. But R9-5-203 requires a **valid card before the staff member's starting date**, so there is no conditional or provisional start while a card is pending. Card lead time must be budgeted into the onboarding funnel.

**The Maricopa County layer is the real operational complexity.** Arizona licensing is state-run, so there is no separate county child care license — but a thirty-site Phoenix-metro portfolio is managing roughly eight to twelve different **municipal** zoning regimes (Phoenix, Mesa, Chandler, Gilbert, Glendale, Scottsdale, Tempe, Peoria, Surprise, Goodyear), each with its own conditional use permit process, plus fire inspection tied to license renewal, business licensing, and environmental health permits under the Maricopa County Environmental Health Code as revised September 10, 2025. **Maricopa publishes food-service inspection results in a public searchable database** — a reputational exposure worth monitoring proactively across all kitchens.

**ADHS enforcement statistics for FY2025–FY2026 are [unconfirmed]** and would be the single most useful missing dataset for benchmarking thirty sites. A public-records request to BCCL for enforcement actions by facility and top-ten cited rule sections would close it.

### Subsidy and CCDF — the waitlist is the dominant fact

The Department of Economic Security reinstated the Child Care Assistance waiting list on **August 1, 2024**.

| Date | Children | Families |
|---|---|---|
| August 2024 (reinstatement) | ~2,000 | — |
| February 28, 2025 | 2,607 | 1,587 |
| June 26, 2026 | 13,244 | 7,916 |
| **July 2026** | **~13,500** | — |

That is a **575 percent increase since reinstatement.** Advocates put the cost of clearing it at $160 million; the legislature has appropriated roughly $90 million over two years, releasing 900 children in July 2025 and 600 more in February 2026 — 1,500 cumulative — while the list grows by roughly 600 a month.

**The arithmetic does not close. Plan for the waitlist to persist and grow through FY2027**, likely exceeding 17,000 to 18,000 by January 2027.

**The waitlist exemptions are the reliable subsidy channel.** Families bypassing the list entirely: Jobs-program referrals; Cash Assistance recipients maintaining employment; Transitional Child Care; **Department of Child Safety or Tribal Child Welfare referrals** with documented case-plan need; siblings in households already receiving DCS-referred care; and families with a DCS or tribal referral within the prior thirty days. **DCS-referred placements are the only subsidy stream in Arizona currently immune to the waitlist, and sites with DCS relationships have a materially more stable subsidy census.** This warrants a deliberate business-development push.

**Eligibility** is set at **165 percent of the federal poverty level** — well below the federal ceiling of 85 percent SMI. Arizona is leaving significant federally permitted eligibility on the table, which is why only about 16 percent of eligible Arizona children receive a subsidy.

**Reimbursement rates, effective November 1, 2025:**

| Age group | Base daily | Quality rate (3–5 star, +40%) |
|---|---|---|
| Infant (under 1) | $64.15 | **$89.81** |
| Toddler (1–2) | $48.70 | $68.18 |
| Preschool (3–5) | $42.00 | $58.80 |
| School-age, summer | $35.00 | $49.00 |
| School-age, school year | $25.00 | $35.00 |

**The methodology contains the central weakness.** Rates are set at the 75th percentile of the **2022** market rate survey for infants under one, and only the **50th percentile** of the 2022 survey for ages one through twelve. Two problems follow: the 50th-percentile benchmark for everything above infancy calibrates the subsidy to the median of a depressed market, and **DES published a 2024 market rate survey that is not being used.** The state minimum wage rose from $13.85 in 2022 to $15.15 in 2026 — roughly 9.4 percent — with no rate rebase.

**This is the strongest, most defensible advocacy argument available in Arizona, and a rate rebase to the 2024 survey is a cleaner and more winnable ask than the $160 million waitlist appropriation the advocacy community is pursuing.**

**The 40 percent quality differential is the single largest revenue lever in the subsidy channel** — worth $25.66 a day per subsidised infant. It applies to providers rated three, four or five stars in Quality First or meeting Head Start standards. Governor Hobbs's FY2027 executive budget explicitly funds "$44.8M GF … to maintain a 40% high quality enhancement," which confirms the differential is a **discretionary, appropriation-dependent policy rather than a permanent entitlement.** It is exposed in any downturn budget and is the easiest line to trim.

**Arizona pays on attendance** — the daily rate applies "when attending for 15 minutes or more." Revenue risk from absenteeism sits with the provider. Arizona is not among the states that adopted enrollment-based payment, and the July 13, 2026 federal rescission removes any pressure to change.

Budget trajectory: FY2025 delivered $12 million General Fund, the first GF appropriation for child care assistance in over a decade. FY2026 enacted $44.9 million — "the largest appropriation since 2009." The FY2027 executive request was $44.8 million; **FY2027 enacted is approximately $48 million.**

### First Things First — a structurally decaying asset

Created by Proposition 203 (2006) and funded by a dedicated tobacco tax, First Things First serves children from birth to five and is governed by the Arizona Early Childhood Development and Health Board. CEO: **Melinda Gulick**.

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| Year-one revenue (FY2008) | ~$180 million |
| Most recent fiscal year | **~$89 million** |
| Decline over 20 years | **~50%** |
| Decline in the last 3 years alone | $32 million |
| ASU estimate of revenue foregone by not taxing vapes | $1.6 billion |

The cause is structural and simple. Proposition 203 taxed cigarettes, cavendish, chewing tobacco and cigars. **Vaping entered the market in 2007 — one year after the vote — and is not covered.** Every smoker who converts is a direct revenue loss.

Because Prop 203 was voter-enacted, its revenue is protected by the Voter Protection Act: the legislature cannot sweep or repurpose it without a three-fourths supermajority and a furtherance-of-purpose finding, or a return to the ballot. **Treat the revenue base as eroding but the legal protection as intact.** First Things First has sought a statutory fix for two consecutive sessions without bipartisan support; the 2025 vehicle, HB 2778, a luxury tax on nicotine and vapor products directing 60 percent to FTF, did not receive a hearing in its chamber of origin.

**A reintroduced vapor-tax-to-FTF bill in 2027 is the most plausible bipartisan child care revenue vehicle in Arizona** — it is a revenue fix for a voter-protected program, framed as closing a loophole rather than raising a tax — and is worth active support.

**One bright spot.** Arizona was among 23 states awarded a **Preschool Development Grant Birth Through Five Systems Building Grant of $8.4 million**, administered through the Department of Education. Roughly $500,000 will let approximately 60 additional providers join Quality First as Rating Only participants, and **beginning July 2027 Rating Only participants at three or more stars become eligible for Quality First Scholarships** previously reserved for Full Support participants.

### State pre-K and the ESA question

**Arizona has no universal or broadly funded state pre-K program.** Public preschool is delivered through a patchwork of federal Head Start and Early Head Start (16,500 children — nine percent of EHS-eligible, 26 percent of HS-eligible), district-run preschool, early childhood special education, and First Things First Quality First Scholarships. Arizona sits near the bottom nationally in state pre-K investment — **which is precisely why the private market carries so much of the three-to-five load, and why First Things First scholarships matter disproportionately.**

**On Empowerment Scholarship Accounts, the answer matters and it is restrictive.** Arizona runs the nation's largest universal school-choice voucher program, but for preschool the door is mostly closed:

- The general rule requires a student to be **at least five years of age as of January 1** of the contract year to qualify as an entering kindergartener. **Typically developing preschoolers aged three and four cannot participate.**
- **The one exception is preschoolers with disabilities.** A child aged three or older, not yet kindergarten age, qualifies with a current MET evaluation report, IEP or 504 plan issued by an Arizona public school — and this category is exempt from the 45-day prior public-school enrollment requirement that gates other pathways.

Three practical conclusions follow. ESA is **not** a general revenue channel for three- and four-year-olds and should not be modelled as one. ESA **is** a viable channel for a defined niche — preschool-age children with a public-school-issued MET, IEP or 504 — which requires family awareness, referral relationships with district early childhood evaluation teams, and operational capacity to accept ESA payment through ClassWallet. And **the five-by-January-1 kindergarten rule creates a genuine ESA-funded transitional-kindergarten opportunity** for older fours and young fives: if any Phoenix-metro site operates a kindergarten or transitional-K program, that program can be ESA-funded where the typical preschool cannot. **This is the most under-exploited ESA angle for the business model.** Award amounts are **[unconfirmed]**.

**ESA politics are live.** The FY2027 budget maintained the program intact. Republicans referred **HCR 2048** to the November 2026 ballot — framed as the "Military Families College Savings and Scholarship Protection Act," carrying a poison pill voiding other ESA reforms — while a competing citizen initiative, the **Protect Education Act**, would impose income limits and spending restrictions. **Both are live on the November 3, 2026 ballot and the ESA landscape could shift materially within ninety days.**

### Quality First

Operated by First Things First across roughly 1,100 to 1,300 participating programs, with about 87 percent of enrolled providers at three stars or above. Two participation tracks: **Full Support** (assessment, rating, coordination plus coaching, health consultation and funding) and **Rating Only** (assessment, rating and coordination, with the program waitlisted for full support). Participation is free to the provider.

Incentives tied to rating include materials, equipment and **facility improvements** — including shelving, fencing, painting and carpet — professional development funds, staff recognition and retention funds, and Quality First Scholarships providing direct tuition subsidy for low-income families.

**The money tie that matters most: the DES 40 percent subsidy rate enhancement is conditioned on a three-, four- or five-star Quality First rating.** Quality First is therefore not a soft branding exercise in Arizona; it is the gate on the highest-value subsidy revenue. **Any site at one or two stars, or not participating at all, is leaving roughly 40 percent of subsidy revenue on the table at that site.**

### 2025–2026 legislation and the 2027 outlook

**The structural dynamic is blunt.** Democratic Governor Katie Hobbs faces a Republican House and Senate under Speaker Steve Montenegro (R-Goodyear). Democratic child care spending bills die without hearings; Republican priorities die by veto; **the budget is the only real vehicle.** Hobbs recorded 151 vetoes in 2026.

In 2025, Rep. Sarah Liguori's HB 2643 (a $120–211 million DES appropriation) and HB 2778 (the vapor tax) both received **no hearing in the chamber of origin**. Rep. Walt Blackman (R-LD7) introduced a $6 million rural child care development center bill and a child care infrastructure grant program; the latter was heard in Senate Federalism & Family Law. In 2026, **Liguori's HB 2648 seeking $160 million to eliminate the waitlist received no committee consideration at all.**

The FY2027 budget, passed June 11, 2026, runs approximately **$18.3 billion General Fund**, a 3.1 percent increase, with approximately $48 million for child care assistance, **full federal tax conformity producing a $1.45 billion tax cut over three years**, and a 2.5 percent across-the-board agency spending reduction.

**The fiscal squeeze is already locked in.** The three-year tax cut and the agency reduction mean FY2028 begins with less room regardless of who wins in November — and child care assistance is a discretionary General Fund line.

The 58th Legislature convenes approximately **January 11, 2027**. The November 3, 2026 general election is the hinge: Hobbs seeks re-election against a Republican field led by Rep. Andy Biggs, with polling through April 2026 at roughly Hobbs 47.1 percent to Biggs 37.6 percent and forecaster ratings from Tossup to Lean Democratic. All 90 legislative seats are on the ballot. **A Hobbs second term means continuity — appropriations in the $45–50 million band, executive-branch workarounds, and a continued veto wall against deregulation. A Biggs administration means discontinuity in both directions: possible ESA expansion into younger ages and licensing deregulation, alongside likely flat or reduced child care appropriations.**

For an operator of this scale that is an opening.

### Business climate

Arizona's minimum wage is **constitutionally indexed to CPI** under Proposition 206 (2016) and reset each January 1 by the Industrial Commission: $14.35 in 2024, $14.70 in 2025, **$15.15 in 2026**, with the 2027 rate announced around September or October 2026.

Three compounding pressures follow. **Increases are automatic with no legislative off-ramp** — Prop 206 is voter-protected, so 3 to 4 percent annual wage escalation belongs in every Arizona pro forma indefinitely. **Prop 206 also mandates earned paid sick time**, a separate and often under-budgeted cost for a high-headcount, high-turnover workforce. And **the wage floor rises faster than the subsidy rate**, which is the structural margin squeeze described above. Flagstaff and Tucson maintain higher local minimums; neither is in Maricopa County, so all thirty sites are on the state rate.

**No state child care tax credit or employer child care incentive was identified as enacted [unconfirmed but likely accurate].** The relevant credit is federal §45F, expanded effective tax year 2026 — and that is a **demand-side opportunity rather than a cost**. Phoenix-metro employers now have a materially better federal credit for subsidising employee child care, and **a B2B employer-partnership channel is the most attractive growth vector in this landscape precisely because it is insulated from the DES waitlist.**

### What to watch

1. **The ADHS 24-hour annual training compliance deadline — August 2026, now.** Audit training records across all thirty sites immediately; this is the most probable near-term citation source in the portfolio.
2. **November 3, 2026 — Governor plus HCR 2048 plus the Protect Education Act.** A Biggs win flips the veto dynamic entirely, and either ESA measure could reshape whether an ESA-funded transitional-K channel becomes more or less viable.
3. **DES response to the July 13, 2026 federal deregulation.** Watch for a CCDF State Plan amendment, particularly any move on copay levels or the 40 percent quality enhancement, both of which now have less federal protection.
4. **The waitlist trajectory into the FY2028 budget cycle.** Agency FY2028 budget requests land in the autumn of 2026 — that is the window to press for a **rate rebase to the 2024 market rate survey**.
5. **The January 1, 2027 minimum wage announcement (September or October 2026) and the FTF vapor-tax bill.** Model the new wage now; support the third-attempt vapor tax as the most plausible bipartisan child care revenue vehicle in the state.

## 3.4 Texas — 23 centers

> **Snapshot.** A comparatively favourable operating environment, and the only state in the portfolio that has already built and paid for the payment architecture the 2024 federal rule required — enrollment-based, prospective, biweekly, funded with a $50 million transition appropriation. The 2025 session was unusually productive, with fifteen child care bills signed. The near-term question is whether TWC retains that architecture now that the federal mandate has lapsed, and the answer will surface in a Chapter 809 rule package whose comment period closed on July 6, 2026.

### Licensing and regulation

Texas HHSC Child Care Regulation administers 26 TAC Chapter 746 for centers, with Chapter 745 covering cross-cutting licensing and background checks.

Ratios under §746.1601: infants under 11 months 1:4; 12–17 months 1:5; 18–23 months 1:9; two-year-olds 1:11; three-year-olds 1:15; **four-year-olds 1:18**; five-year-olds 1:22; six and older 1:26. **Maximum group sizes are [unconfirmed]** — every retrieval path returned the ratio column only. Texas ratios are notably permissive versus national norms, and 1:18 for four-year-olds is among the loosest in the country, so **group sizes should be confirmed directly from the HHSC Chapter 746 PDF before modelling classroom capacity.**

Directors require 30 clock hours of training annually; caregivers 24 clock hours, with self-study capped at three hours a year.

**Background checks changed materially in 2026 and this is an action item.** HHSC adopted rules implementing **SB 1849, creating SEMARC — the Search Engine for Multi-Agency Reportable Conduct** — pulling findings from the Texas Education Agency, the Texas Juvenile Justice Department and the HHSC Employee Misconduct Registry into the background check process. Adopted in the Texas Register on **July 17, 2026**. **HR onboarding and any third-party screening vendor must be SEMARC-ready;** misses become licensing deficiencies, which feed Texas Rising Star scoring, which feeds rates.

Inspection frequency: at least one unannounced monitoring inspection every six months in the first twelve months with a full standards evaluation within twelve months; thereafter at least annually and not more than 366 days apart, unannounced and risk-based. **Deficiency count and weighting drive increased frequency — and the same weighted-deficiency score gates Texas Rising Star entry.** Licensing compliance and subsidy revenue are directly linked in Texas.

Also adopted July 2026: **HB 4529** (military-operated child care licensing exemption) and **HB 3597** (raising the population threshold for a public-hearing requirement from 300,000 to 500,000).

**Enforcement trend data is [unconfirmed].**

### Subsidy and CCDF

The lead agency is the Texas Workforce Commission, delivering through **28 Local Workforce Development Boards**. Boards set local rates within TWC parameters, so **rates and policies vary by board** — a key operational fact for a multi-site operator.

**Rate methodology, per WD Letter 08-25, effective October 1, 2025:** boards set rates at the **higher of** the 75th percentile of the 2025 Texas Child Care Market Rate Survey **or** baseline rates from the Cost of Quality Price Modeling Report. **That "whichever is higher" floor is a genuine alternative-methodology protection and is more provider-favourable than most states in the portfolio.** Rates are set across eight age ranges per board. **Dollar-level rates by board are [unconfirmed]** — TWC publishes them per board via TX3C.

Texas Rising Star enhanced rates above base: **4-star +9 percent, 3-star +7 percent, 2-star +5 percent**, with a minimum two-percentage-point differential between consecutive levels.

**Texas pays on enrollment, prospectively, every two weeks.** Per WD 06-24 Chapter 2, effective December 9, 2024, TWC pays regulated providers prospectively on authorised enrollment, not attendance, and **allocated $50 million to fund the transition.**

Copays run on a sliding scale from two percent of gross income at one percent SMI to a **seven percent cap** at 76–85 percent SMI, applying per additional child. Zero-calculated copays may not be charged. TWC discontinued the Texas Rising Star provider copay discount in December 2024, grandfathering existing recipients.

Eligibility runs to **85 percent of state median income** with a twelve-month minimum eligibility period. **SB 462 (2025) adds children of child care workers to waitlist priority effective September 1, 2025** — a direct staff-recruitment lever against a waitlist of roughly 95,000 to 100,000 children.

Scale context: **$1.4 billion in Child Care Services funding serving approximately 150,000 children daily** against roughly 1.2 million licensed slots statewide. Only 50 percent of regulated programs accept subsidy, and 60 percent of participating providers have subsidy children below 20 percent of capacity. Roughly 87.5 percent of the Texas market is private pay.

**The central open question.** Texas already built the 2024 rule's architecture and spent $50 million doing it. The federal floor disappeared on July 13, 2026 but the rescission does not prohibit states from continuing, and **Texas has issued no public signal of reversal [unconfirmed]**. Sunk cost and the TX3C build argue for retention. The **40 TAC Chapter 809 rule package is where any change would surface** — the Commission approved proposed amendments on May 19, 2026 to "streamline certain rules, simplify Texas Rising Star parameters, and make programmatic improvements," **public comment closed July 6, 2026, and adoption is imminent.** The redline could not be retrieved. **This is the highest-priority document to obtain in the Texas file.**

**A separate compliance tightening independent of payment basis.** WD 06-26 Change 1 mandates **dual check-in and check-out** via the TX3C suite (KinderConnect, KinderSign, KinderSmart, KinderBridge). A missing scan counts as an unexplained absence. Non-compliance carries a **one-week cure window, then payment withholding** and possible contract termination. Absence thresholds sit at 15, 30 and 40, with more than 40 in twelve months permitting eligibility termination. **A one-week leash across a multi-site footprint is unusually short and warrants a dedicated operational control.**

### State pre-K

Texas funds half-day pre-K for eligible four-year-olds through the Foundation School Program plus an Early Education Allotment of roughly $800 million. **HB 2 (2025)** — Rep. Brad Buckley's $8.5 billion school finance package — restructured the allotment so half-ADA for eligible four-year-old pre-K students is funded first, generating roughly $90 million additional for early literacy and math. It also newly qualified **children of public school teachers**.

**Mixed delivery under HB 2 is a mixed picture:**

- HB 2 **mandates an 85 percent passthrough** of partnership funds to private providers.
- New intermediary oversight bodies must **verify that no eligible private child care is available before a district builds new pre-K classrooms** — a meaningful anti-crowd-out protection.
- But HB 2 **removed full-day ADA funding protection** for partnership students, eroding partnership economics.
- **Tuition-based pre-K faces new restrictions after 2027.**

For-profit providers can contract via TEA public-private prekindergarten partnerships and TWC Prekindergarten Partnerships. **Per-child partnership funding is [unconfirmed].**

**SB 2 (2025), the ESA program**, begins in the 2026-27 school year with eligible pre-K children receiving accounts above $10,000 (up to $30,000 for special needs), usable at private schools **or approved child care**, against a $1 billion appropriation with a lottery prioritising low-income and special-needs children. **This is a potential new private-pay revenue channel**, and the House Public Education interim charge explicitly studies its effect on private child care providers.

### Texas Rising Star

**Mandatory for subsidy since October 3, 2022.** Levels run Entry → 2-Star → 3-Star → 4-Star.

**The entry gate is licensing compliance:** no more than 40 points of high or medium-high weighted deficiencies over six months. Entry-level programs have 24 months to reach at least two stars. Weighting: Teacher-Child Interactions 40 percent; Director and Staff Qualifications 20 percent; Program Administration 20 percent; Indoor and Outdoor Environments 20 percent. Certification runs three years with two unannounced monitoring visits.

**Texas Rising Star is the hinge connecting three separate benefit streams** — subsidy rate enhancement, the property tax exemption, and licensing standing — and simplification of its parameters is pending in the Chapter 809 package.

### Business climate

## 3.5 Florida — 16 centers

> **Snapshot.** Florida's Voluntary Prekindergarten programme is not a market a private operator participates in at the margin — **it is a privately delivered public programme, with 77 percent of VPK providers private and licensed private centers alone accounting for 68 percent of the provider base.** That is the strongest political asset in the state. It sits alongside the least favourable subsidy terms in the portfolio and the single sharpest cost-versus-revenue divergence anywhere in this report: **the VPK base student allocation has been flat at $3,029 for three consecutive fiscal years while the state minimum wage rises 25 percent.**

### Licensing and regulation

**Two agencies split the file, and this is the most common error in vendor briefings.** Licensing sits with the **Department of Children and Families**. Subsidy and VPK sit with the **Division of Early Learning inside the Department of Education**, operating through 30 early learning coalitions plus the Redlands Christian Migrant Association. The 2021–23 restructuring moved the former Office of Early Learning into FLDOE; **it did not move licensing.**

DCF administers licensing in **63 of 67 counties. Broward, Palm Beach, Pinellas and Sarasota operate their own local licensing agencies** — meaning a multi-site operator in those counties faces separate inspection regimes with independent enforcement discretion.

Ratios under s. 402.305(4), F.S.: under 1 year 1:4; 1 to under 2 1:6; 2 to under 3 1:11; 3 to under 4 1:15; **4 to under 5 1:20**; 5 and older 1:25.

**Florida imposes no separate statutory maximum group sizes. Capacity is governed by square footage plus ratio.** This is a real competitive advantage relative to benchmark states, and it partly explains why NIEER scored Florida only four of ten quality benchmarks while ranking it **second nationally on access** for four-year-olds.

Staff requirements: a 40-clock-hour introductory course within one year of start; annual in-service of one CEU or ten clock hours; early literacy of 0.5 CEU or five clock hours once during employment; a Director Credential as a minimum licensing standard; and one credentialed staff member per twenty children.

Level 2 background screening includes employment history, criminal history, sexual predator and offender registries, and **the child abuse and neglect registry of every state of residence in the preceding five years**, with DCF required to complete screening within three business days. **CS/SB 738 (2025) created a 45-day provisional hiring status** during pending screening, with supervision requirements — a material hiring-velocity improvement.

Enforcement under s. 402.310 permits fines up to $100 per violation per day, or up to $500 per day where the violation could result in death or serious harm; probation up to six months, non-renewable; and denial, suspension or revocation. **CS/SB 738 created a three-tier violation classification (Class 1/2/3)** effective July 1, 2025 — the enforcement grid center directors are now managed against. **Inspection frequency under s. 402.311 is [unconfirmed].**

**CS/CS/CS/SB 1690 (2026), Chapter 2026-140**, sponsored by Sen. Calatayud and passed 37-0 and 111-0, took effect **July 1, 2026**. It renames "family day care home" to "family child care home"; **requires license-exempt providers — church and parochial schools, employer-operated centers — to disclose exempt status on their websites and materials**, a competitive-transparency win for licensed operators; extends residential property insurance non-cancellation protection to large family child care homes; creates the **Florida Child Care Fund** inside FLDOE's direct-support organisation; expands the TEACH Scholarship and creates a Center for Early Childhood Professional Recognition; and requires **Division of Early Learning annual reporting beginning January 1, 2027.**

### Subsidy — School Readiness

FY2024-25 actuals: **210,786 children served across 6,919 providers**, average monthly enrollment 153,804, **waitlist peaking at 18,948 in June 2025**, total expenditures $1.21 billion with 74.78 percent to direct services. The Florida Policy Institute estimates only about **15 percent of eligible families** receive a voucher.

Rates are set by county, provider type, unit of care and age band, adopted annually by the Legislature in the appropriations process. Illustrative FY2025-26 statewide base rates for licensed and exempt providers: full-time infant **$50.00 a day**; full-time preschool three-year-old $27.95; part-time school-age $15.33. County variance is wide — Miami-Dade full-time infant is $55.20.

**Methodology and the gap.** Florida uses a **narrow cost analysis, not a cost estimation model.** Current rates sit **below** the federal 75th-percentile equal-access benchmark — Gold Seal infant at approximately **88.60 percent** and non-Gold Seal infant at approximately **84.90 percent** of the 75th percentile. Gold Seal carries a minimum 20 percent differential; quality performance incentives add four to ten percent.

**The payment-practice question is the most consequential item in the Florida file, and it moved in the last sixty days.**

The sequence matters. **Florida took four ACF waivers from the 2024 CCDF rule — the 7 percent copay cap, prospective payment (explicitly not eligible for renewal), enrollment-based payment, and targeted grants and contracts — all expiring August 1, 2026.** Florida's CCDF appendix committed to amend Rule 6M-4.500 by April 30, 2026 to require payment prior to delivery of services and payment based on enrollment, implement by July 1, 2026, and file a plan amendment by July 31, 2026. Florida acknowledged in that filing that it **currently exceeds the 7 percent copay cap and permits providers to charge families above the copay.**

Then the federal floor was pulled out on July 13, 2026.

**And the only 2026 amendment to Rule 6M-4.500, effective June 17, 2026, concerns the special-needs differential — not prospective or enrollment-based payment.** The rulemaking trail is likewise special-needs only.

**The conclusion, high-confidence but formally [unconfirmed]: Florida appears to have let the enrollment-based and prospective payment reforms lapse rather than implement them, and the federal rescission ratified that outcome three weeks ago. Florida remains an attendance-based, arrears-payment state.** Confirm by reading the current 6M-4.500 text and the Division's July 31, 2026 plan amendment filing. If correct, it removes a working-capital improvement competitors in enrollment-pay states are already booking, and it is the strongest available ask for the 2027 session.

**Eligibility.** CS/CS/HB 1255 (2025), Chapter 2025-110, effective July 1, 2025, redefined "economically disadvantaged" as families at or below **55 percent of state median income**, replacing the 150-percent-of-FPL standard, and required waitlists to track children by household income and priority level. A parallel 50-percent-SMI version, CS/HB 859, passed the House 114-0 and Senate 38-0 but died in returning messages on June 16, 2025; its staff analysis warned that without new appropriation the effect of eligibility expansion is **a longer waitlist, not more enrollment.**

### Quality and accountability

Florida runs a single seven-point observational programme assessment across both School Readiness and VPK, and **it is a contract eligibility gate, not a star rating.**

Under s. 1002.68, F.S., VPK programme assessment measures emotional support, classroom organisation and instructional support, with the composite weighted at 50 percent or more of provider designations across five tiers. Providers below minimum go on probation; failure, or **two consecutive years on probation, triggers removal from eligibility for two to five years.**

**The number that binds is 4.00.** Under F.A.C. 6M-8.621, a VPK contract minimum composite below 4.00 means the provider cannot participate in the consecutive programme year and thereafter until the threshold is met, with one self-funded re-assessment permitted within 30 days. Under 6M-4.740, the same 4.00 threshold applies to School Readiness, with failure triggering a Quality Improvement Plan if the provider is deemed essential to local capacity, and otherwise contract termination and up to five-year eligibility revocation.

**But in FY2024-25, 99 percent of assessed providers scored at or above 4.00 in both programmes**, with average composites of 5.23 and 5.31. **A 99 percent pass rate means the threshold is a floor, not a screen.** For a well-run operator the risk is not failing; it is that the differential is capped at 15 percent while the base rate sits 11 to 15 percent below the 75th percentile. **Quality dollars cannot close a base-rate gap. Any 2027 strategy that leads with "raise the quality differential" is arguing for the smaller of the two pools.**

Gold Seal designation covered 1,934 providers in FY2024-25, of whom 1,589 contracted for School Readiness and 1,648 for VPK.

## 3.6 Arkansas — 14 centers

> **Snapshot.** The most urgent item in this entire report sits in Arkansas. **The state's child care sustainability funding package expires on September 30, 2026 — roughly eight weeks from now — with no successor funding decision in the public record, no legislative session until January 2027, and a 2026 fiscal session that declined to add early childhood money.** Any rate reduction or eligibility tightening will arrive by administrative action in the fourth quarter of 2026 with minimal notice.

### Licensing and regulation

**Child care licensing in Arkansas sits with the Department of Education — Division of Elementary and Secondary Education, Office of Early Childhood — not the Department of Human Services.** The 2023 LEARNS Act, following Governor Sanders's January 11, 2023 executive order, moved the office out of DHS. **This is a genuinely different regulator personality than in most states: an education agency, outcome- and kindergarten-readiness oriented, running licensing.**

**A stale-artifact risk worth flagging to center directors.** Legacy DHS pages remain live, and **DESE's own Child Care Assistance page still instructs applicants to mail DHS at "P.O. Box 1437, Slot S145" and links a 2021 sliding fee chart.** All compliance questions should route to the Office of Early Childhood and the School Readiness Assistance portal.

Ratios and group sizes under the Minimum Licensing Requirements for Child Care Centers:

| Age | Ratio | Max group |
|---|---|---|
| Birth – 18 months | 1:5 | 10 |
| 18 – 36 months | 1:8 | 16 |
| 2.5 – 3 years | 1:12 | 24 |
| 4 years | **1:15** | 30 |
| 5 years – kindergarten | 1:18 | 36 |

Group size is capped at twice the number of children allowed with one staff member. **Infant and toddler ratios of 1:5 and 1:8 are more permissive than the national median and materially better for unit economics than neighbouring states with 1:4 infant rules. This is a labor-cost advantage worth protecting — and worth watching, because any quality push in 2027 will target exactly these numbers.**

Directors must be at least 21 with one of four qualification pathways, and **must be on site at least 50 percent of the center's primary operational day on a routine basis** — a real constraint on roving or shared leadership models across fourteen centers. Staff must be 18 (16 and 17 permitted only under direct supervision) with a high school diploma or GED. Training: 15 clock hours a year for directors and direct care staff; new staff require 8 hours of orientation plus 15 hours of job-specific training within three months.

Background checks require FBI and Arkansas State Police checks every five years and Child Maltreatment Registry checks every two years.

**The enforcement leverage runs through subsidy, and it is unusually severe.** The CCDF participant agreement provides for **immediate termination of subsidy participation upon license revocation, probationary or provisional license status, loss of Better Beginnings Level 2, or federal exclusion-list placement.** **A provisional license — not merely a revocation — cuts off subsidy revenue.** In a state where subsidy is a meaningful revenue line, that is a severe cliff and a strong argument for over-investing in licensing compliance.

**The operative center licensing rulebook available through the federal database remains the December 2020 edition, and whether the Office of Early Childhood has promulgated a comprehensive post-transfer rewrite is [unconfirmed]** — the largest verification gap in the Arkansas file. The Arkansas Early Childhood Commission holds rule-approval authority; **its published minutes run only through April 2025.**

**A licensing-reform report is already on the table.** United WE commissioned the University of Arkansas Office for Education Policy, with additional Walton Family Foundation funding, for a year-long study released October 29, 2025 with the policy brief following November 5. Method: 32 providers in focus groups and more than 50 expert interviews. **Provider satisfaction with licensing averaged 5.7 out of 10**, with extensive documentation, **overlapping inspections** and long approval periods the chief complaints. Six recommendations: streamline licensing and align state and local requirements; financial support including subsidies, insurance options and facility-upgrade grants; workforce development; **modernise zoning to permit child care in residential areas and establish "Child Care Zones"**; diversify funding beyond federal dependence; and recruit family child care providers.

**This is a foundation-backed, bipartisan-framed, ready-made legislative package and the reform most aligned with operator interests and most likely to pass in 2027.**

### Subsidy — School Readiness Assistance

The Child Care Assistance Program is now **School Readiness Assistance**, on a dedicated portal launched November 18, 2024.

Rates are set from the Arkansas Market Rate Survey at the **75th percentile**, standardised statewide **except Benton and Washington counties in Northwest Arkansas, which carry a higher rate.** A family of three with a child under four and income below $30,334 qualifies for up to **$35 a day**. **The complete county, age and quality-level rate table is not publicly posted [unconfirmed]** and should be obtained through the provider portal.

**The 2025 restructuring collapsed payment tiers so that rates are identical for Better Beginnings levels 3–4 and identical for levels 5–6** — designed to avoid direct payment cuts while preserving quality incentives.

**Payment basis is attendance-anchored** — the participant agreement reimburses for "services delivered" — but Arkansas softens it with a generous absence allowance rather than true enrollment-based payment: six days a month and twelve per period in July–October and March–June, eight and sixteen in November–February, with an **annual ceiling of 40 days** absent a written waiver. **Up to five paid professional development days a year** and holiday billing when the facility is closed and the child was scheduled are also permitted.

**One operational risk deserves direct attention: "OEC is not obligated to pay bills more than thirty calendar days after the date of service" absent prior written approval. Late submission means forfeited revenue.** Verify that the Arkansas billing cycle clears this comfortably.

Eligibility runs on monthly income limits effective October 1, 2025 keyed to state median income, with children birth through twelve, family assets under $1 million, and parents working or in school. **The work requirement was raised from 20 to 30 hours a week in November 2025** — a demand-side headwind that will disqualify part-time-working parents and shrink subsidy-funded census at the margin. The 72-month lifetime limit was eliminated, partially offsetting.

Copays are zero for families at or below the state poverty level. **Uniform copayment percentages across age groups for multi-child families were adopted; capping multi-child copays at 7 percent of household income and eliminating copays below 60 percent SMI remained under consideration as of November 3, 2025, at an estimated cost of $5 to $7 million. Whether Arkansas adopted the 7 percent cap between November 2025 and August 2026 is [unconfirmed] and is a high priority** — it was a federal requirement until July 13, 2026, and Arkansas's hedging suggests a compliance gap that the rescission may simply have resolved by default.

**The cliff.** The October–November 2025 sustainability package — approximately **$77 million through September 30, 2026**, following an emergency bridge of $28 million to sustain programs for eight weeks — was adopted after **250 child care facilities across 50 of Arkansas's 75 counties faced potential closure** and roughly 400 layoffs were projected. Deputy Commissioner Stacy Smith called the plan "still doable," requiring monthly financial monitoring.

**Arkansas allocates roughly $101 million in federal CCDF against approximately $7.8 million in state funds — a state share of roughly 7 percent, an unusually thin state commitment.** That is precisely the "diversify funding beyond federal dependence" recommendation in the United WE report, and precisely why the FY2027 cliff is dangerous.

Enrollment: more than 16,000 children, with roughly 1,100 on the waitlist as of February 2025.

### Arkansas Better Chance

Established in 1991 and expanded in 2003, ABC serves birth to five with developmental and socio-economic risk factors across center-based, home-visiting and family child care home models. **Private and for-profit centers are eligible** — the 2025–26 finance guidance explicitly distinguishes LEAs from "private programs" with different administrative cost caps.

**But size the opportunity carefully.** Rep. Julie Mayberry stated in April 2026 that "most ABC providers operate within public school systems," arguing 75 percent of any appropriation increase would flow back to public schools. **The private and for-profit share of ABC slots is [unconfirmed] and appears to be a minority.** Obtain the provider-type breakdown from the Office of Early Childhood before sizing.

2025–26 funding rates: **center-based $5,105 per child per year**; home-visiting $1,894. Terms include a **40 percent match requirement** (ABC reimburses 60 percent, and match may be cash or in-kind including donated goods, services, equipment, utilities and labor), administrative cost caps of 12 percent for LEAs and **15 percent for private programs**, monthly payment October through April based on enrollment, and a **separate bank account with no commingling.**

**At $5,105 per child per year with a 40 percent match obligation and a 15 percent admin cap, ABC is a thin-margin-to-loss-leader programme for a for-profit operator absent a strong in-kind match strategy.** The in-kind flexibility is the key lever. ABC's quality standards also impose 1:10 ratios and one bachelor's degree holder per three classrooms against a licensing baseline of 1:15 with no degree requirement — the staffing delta belongs in any pro forma.

Statewide 2024-25: **20,327 children enrolled — up just 16 from the prior year** — against $106.1 million state spending plus $7.5 million TANF and **$14.0 million in federal recovery funds now expiring.** State spending per child $6,278, up nine percent inflation-adjusted, meeting eight of ten NIEER benchmarks. **Note the tension: the increase went to per-child adequacy and to backfilling expiring federal money, not to slot expansion — and a second cliff is embedded in the ABC number.**

**Act 504 of 2025 (HB1733)**, approved April 10, 2025, consolidated the Arkansas Better Chance and ABC for School Success programmes across approximately 23,000 slots, repealing the prior 200-percent-of-poverty eligibility definition and letting the department set unified eligibility on risk factors including **family income, parental education level, low birth weight, limited English proficiency and developmental delays.** **This broadens the eligible population beyond a pure income test and expands the addressable ABC-eligible census in mixed-income centers.**

**The April 2026 rate fight was lost — but on funding source, not merits.** Rep. Julie Mayberry (R-Hensley) brought a proposal to appropriate roughly **$22 million** from the public school adequacy fund and require ABC providers be reimbursed at no less than 75 percent of current market rates. Her case: **ABC pays $28 a day against $33 to $36 for other early childhood programmes; ABC has not had a significant increase in funding since 2008;** 70 percent of participating pre-K students reached kindergarten readiness. **Sen. Jonathan Dismang objected to drawing from the public school adequacy fund** given concerns that Educational Freedom Accounts are already straining public education. **A 2027 ABC increase needs a funding source outside the adequacy fund — general revenue, TANF or surplus. That is the coalition design problem to solve before January 2027.**

### Better Beginnings

**The scale is now 1 to 6, not 1 to 3** — the public database lets parents filter by ratings on a 1–6 scale and the 2025 rate restructuring explicitly references quality levels 3–4 and 5–6. **But the public Better Beginnings site still publishes only Level One, Two and Three Star Kits; the published requirements for levels 4, 5 and 6 are [unconfirmed] and not publicly documented.** This is a material gap for any rating-advancement strategy and should be requested directly.

Two hard linkages: **loss of Level 2 triggers immediate termination from subsidy participation** — Level 2 is the price of admission — and reimbursement is tiered by level, with the 2025 restructuring collapsing 3–4 and 5–6.

**The tier collapse destroys the marginal return on moving from 3→4 and from 5→6. The only rate-relevant moves are 2→3 and 4→5.** Any quality-investment plan should be re-sequenced accordingly.

**A reputational note.** The Childcare Arkansas database, launched July 8, 2025 under the LEARNS Act, makes ratings, hours, transportation, voucher acceptance and **complaint history** consumer-facing and map-searchable, and supports filing complaints against facilities. **Audit Arkansas listings for accuracy — errors in a state-run parent-choice tool are a direct enrollment risk.**

> **A correction that should be circulated internally.** A commercial vendor site markets an "Arkansas Employer Child Care Tax Credit 2026 | Act 1435 — $15M Annual Cap," claiming a 30 percent credit, a $3,600-per-child cap, a $500,000 per-employer cap and a $3.75 million rural set-aside, and describes it as "active for the 2026 program year." **The Arkansas General Assembly's own bill record shows HB1435 died in House committee at sine die on May 5, 2025 and was never assigned an act number.** The same page attributes oversight to an entity that has not held the portfolio since the 2023 LEARNS transfer. **Do not build tax planning on it.**

Credits that do exist in Arkansas code: **Ark. Code §§ 26-51-507 and 26-51-508**, an income tax credit of **3.9 percent of the annual salary** of employees exclusively providing child care services, with a two-year carryforward; and **§§ 26-52-516 and 26-53-132**, a **refund of gross receipts tax paid on construction materials and furnishings** for employer-operated or employer-contracted child care facilities. **The construction-materials refund is directly usable in new-center capital expenditure.** Verify current statutory text with the Department of Finance and Administration before claiming.

### Business climate

Average annual child care cost **$9,178**; a family with two young children pays **$17,500 a year — 27 percent of median household income.** Estimated annual economic loss from inadequate child care: **$1.2 billion.** Arkansas ranks 43rd on KIDS COUNT child well-being.

Arkansas women's employment rate is 76.5 percent against 79 percent nationally; **69 percent of surveyed mothers cited child care cost as a critical employment barrier.** Anna Koelsch of the Women's Foundation put it usefully: *"When the cost of care rivals or exceeds what a second income brings home, leaving the workforce is a rational financial decision."* **That reframes subsidy as labor-force policy rather than welfare and is the single most useful advocacy quote in the Arkansas record.**

**Zoning is identified as a primary structural barrier** — restricting residential and mixed-use locations, with variation across municipalities creating unpredictability for multi-site expansion. **No statewide zoning preemption exists.** A 2027 preemption bill would be a direct expansion enabler.
