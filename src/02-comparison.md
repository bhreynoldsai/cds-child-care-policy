## 2.2 Licensing Ratios and Group Sizes

Ratios drive labor cost more directly than any other regulatory variable. The table below is ordered from most to least permissive at age four.

| State | Infant | 1 yr | 2 yr | 3 yr | 4 yr | 5 yr | School age | Group size caps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Georgia** | 1:6 | 1:8 | 1:10 | 1:15 | **1:18** | 1:20 | 1:25 | Yes — 12/16/20/30/36/40/50 |
| **Texas** | 1:4 | 1:5 (12–17mo), 1:9 (18–23mo) | 1:11 | 1:15 | **1:18** | 1:22 | 1:26 | **[unconfirmed]** |
| **Alabama** | 1:5 | 1:7 | 1:8 | 1:11 (2.5–4 yr) | **1:18** | 1:18 | 1:21–22 | None published |
| **Arkansas** | 1:5 | 1:8 | 1:12 (2.5–3 yr) | 1:12 | **1:15** | 1:18 | 1:18 | Yes — 2× the ratio |
| **Arizona** | 1:5 | 1:6 | 1:8 | 1:13 | **1:15** | 1:20 | 1:20 | **None** — youngest child sets ratio |
| **Tennessee** | 1:4 | 1:6 | 1:7 | 1:9 | **1:13** | 1:16 | 1:20 | Yes — 8/12/14/16/18/20 |
| **Kentucky** | 1:5 | 1:6 | 1:10 | 1:12 | **1:14** | 1:15 | 1:20–25 | Yes (Type I only) — 10/12/20/24/28/30 |
| **North Carolina** | 1:5 | 1:6 | 1:10 | 1:15 | **1:20** | 1:25 | 1:25 | Yes — 10/12/20/25/25/25 |
| **Florida** | 1:4 | 1:6 | 1:11 | 1:15 | **1:20** | 1:25 | 1:25 | **None** — square footage governs |
| **Oklahoma** | 1:4 | 1:5 | 1:5 | 1:10 | **1:10** | 1:10 | — | **[unconfirmed]** |
| **Virginia** | 1:4 | 1:5 (16–24mo) | 1:8 | 1:10 | **1:10** | 1:10 | 1:18–20 | Only school-age (max 100) |

**Reading the table.**

North Carolina and Florida both permit 1:20 for four-year-olds, but North Carolina caps the group at 25 while **Florida imposes no group-size cap at all** — capacity in Florida is governed by square footage plus ratio. Combined with Florida's second-place national ranking on pre-K access and only four of ten NIEER quality benchmarks, this makes Florida the most revenue-per-square-foot-flexible state in the portfolio.

**Arizona likewise imposes no hard group-size cap.** The binding constraint is the mixed-age rule: the age of the youngest child in the group sets the ratio for the whole group. That is an operational scheduling problem, not a capacity ceiling.

**Georgia's 1:15 at age three and 1:18 at age four are among the most permissive in the country**, and Georgia is 28.5 percent of the portfolio. This is a material and under-appreciated margin position. It is also a standing target: Georgia's own state pre-K program runs at 1:10 with a maximum class size of 20, and every advocacy organization in the state can point at the gap.

**Virginia and Oklahoma are the tightest.** Virginia's 1:10 for three-, four- and five-year-olds is a genuine cost disadvantage relative to the Southeast, partially offset by the loosened school-age ratio under the new licensing chapter. Oklahoma's ratios are drawn from a secondary source and should be verified against Appendix GG before any staffing model relies on them.

**North Carolina's 2025 regulatory reform raised group sizes while leaving ratios untouched** — a deliberate framing choice by sponsors to blunt safety objections, and a template other states are likely to copy.

## 2.4 Quality Rating Systems and the Rate Link

The financial connection between quality rating and subsidy revenue varies enormously across the footprint, and in three states it changed materially in the last year.

| State | System | Participation | Rate differential |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oklahoma** | Reaching for the Stars (5 levels) | Effectively mandatory for subsidy | **Steepest in the portfolio.** Infant daily rate: $20.00 at 1★ → $50.60 at 5★. **The 2★→3★ step alone is +67%** |
| **Texas** | Texas Rising Star (Entry, 2★, 3★, 4★) | **Mandatory** for all subsidy programs since October 3, 2022 | +5% / +7% / +9%. Entry gate is licensing compliance: no more than 40 points of weighted deficiencies over six months |
| **Arizona** | Quality First (1–5 stars) | Voluntary, free to the provider | **+40% for 3★ and above.** On infants that is **$25.66/day** per subsidized child |
| **Alabama** | Quality STARS (5 levels) | 1★ automatic on licensure unless the program opts out | +2% / +4% / +6% / +8% / +10%. Also **gates both state tax credits** |
| **Tennessee** | Report Card (mandatory) + Star-Quality (voluntary) | Report Card mandatory for every licensed agency; four evaluation visits a year | **Cut effective January 1, 2026:** 80–89 score fell from +15% to **+5%**; 90–100 fell from +20% to **+10%** |
| **Kentucky** | All STARS (5 levels) | **Mandatory** for any provider receiving public funds | Monthly per-child add-on: Type I center **$25–$45/mo** under 3, **$23–$43/mo** age 3+, Levels 3–5 |
| **Georgia** | Quality Rated (1–3 stars) | **Mandatory for CAPS funding since December 31, 2021** | Tiered reimbursement plus a Quality Rated Workforce Bonus and Commitment to Quality payments |
| **North Carolina** | Star Rated License (1–5) → "Pathways to the Stars" since July 1, 2025 | Mandatory — every licensed program carries a rating | Rates published by county **and star level**. **Decoupling from reimbursement was directed for study, plan due May 1, 2026** |
| **Florida** | CLASS-based program assessment; Gold Seal | Contract eligibility gate, not a star rating | Minimum composite **4.00** to contract. Gold Seal minimum 20% differential; quality incentive 4–10%; supplement capped at 15% |
| **Arkansas** | Better Beginnings (now 1–6) | **Level 2 is the price of admission** — losing it terminates subsidy participation immediately | **Tiers collapsed in 2025:** levels 3–4 now pay identically, as do levels 5–6 |
| **Virginia** | VQB5 | **Mandatory for any site receiving public funds** — one subsidy child puts the site in | No rate differential. Public quality profiles posted annually |

### Three findings worth acting on

**Tennessee has substantially flattened the return on quality investment.** The January 2026 rate table raised base rates by 10 percent while cutting the quality bonus from 15/20 percent to 5/10 percent. Modeled on a top-tier-county infant at a 90–100 score: $260 × 1.20 = $312.00 in October 2024, versus $286 × 1.10 = $314.60 in January 2026 — **an increase of 0.8 percent.** A provider earning no quality bonus at all received the full 10 percent. The remaining business case for three-star status in Tennessee is **Voluntary Pre-K contracting eligibility**, which carries a hard three-star floor across 138 districts, not subsidy economics.

**Arkansas destroyed the marginal return on two of four possible tier moves.** With levels 3–4 collapsed into one rate and 5–6 into another, the only rate-relevant advancement steps are 2→3 and 4→5. Any quality-investment sequencing in Arkansas should be re-planned accordingly.

**North Carolina's decoupling study is the largest single quality-related revenue risk in the portfolio.** SL 2025-36 directed DCDEE to submit, by May 1, 2026, a plan to separate the quality rating system from subsidy reimbursement rates. Whether that report was delivered and what it recommends is **[unconfirmed]** and is a priority retrieval. If decoupling advances, a high-star operator in 77 North Carolina centers loses its rate premium — and North Carolina is the largest single state position in the portfolio.

**One structural note on Virginia.** VQB5 is unusual nationally: accepting a single subsidy child at a site places that site in a mandatory, publicly reported quality measurement system with no offsetting rate differential. Seven hundred of the eight hundred available points come from CLASS interaction scores, which means there is no compliance-paperwork route to a good rating. The one cheap win is curriculum: 100 points are available simply for using a VDOE-approved curriculum in every classroom and reporting it by October 1.

## 2.5 State Pre-K — The Mixed-Delivery Opportunity

Public pre-K is simultaneously the largest public revenue opportunity and the largest competitive threat in the portfolio, and which of the two it is depends entirely on the state's delivery model.

| State | Program | Private provider role | Per-child or per-classroom funding | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Florida** | VPK | **77% of VPK providers are private; licensed private centers alone are 68%** | $3,029 school-year / $2,586 summer per FTE — **flat for three consecutive years** | **The delivery system.** Political asset, poor rate |
| **Georgia** | Georgia Pre-K (lottery) | **Roughly half of enrollment**, private and community providers | ~$7,630 state spending per child; $30,000 startup grant per new classroom | **Strongest position in the portfolio.** 10 of 10 NIEER benchmarks |
| **Alabama** | First Class Pre-K | Explicitly funded in private child care centers; **auspice breakdown [unconfirmed]** | **$84,804–$97,908/classroom** annually, **up to $125,000 setup**, 25% local match | **Largest untapped opportunity.** 20 straight years at 10 of 10 benchmarks |
| **Virginia** | VPI + **Mixed Delivery Grant Program** | Mixed Delivery is **purpose-built for licensed non-faith-based private settings** | Mixed Delivery re-benchmarked at **$19,441/slot** vs. VPI at $6,763 | **Best-priced public revenue in the portfolio.** Contract-based, cost-of-quality, ~2× oversubscribed |
| **North Carolina** | NC Pre-K | Mixed delivery including private and for-profit centers | ~$7,117 state spending per child | **No new funding** in the FY2026–27 budget; added an unfunded dyslexia screening mandate |
| **Texas** | Half-day pre-K via the Foundation School Program | Public-private partnerships permitted; **HB 2 mandates 85% passthrough** to private providers | Per-child partnership funding **[unconfirmed]** | **Mixed.** HB 2 added anti-crowd-out protection but removed full-day ADA funding for partnership students |
| **Arkansas** | Arkansas Better Chance | Private centers eligible; **most providers operate within public school systems** | **$5,105/child/year** center-based, **40% match required**, 15% admin cap | **Thin to loss-leading** absent a strong in-kind match strategy |
| **Kentucky** | Kentucky Preschool Program | 704 KAR 3:410 **expressly authorizes district contracts** with private preschools | ~$170M over the FY27–28 biennium, frozen at 2019 levels | **Under-exploited.** Kentucky serves 26% of four-year-olds and lags every mixed-delivery neighbor |
| **Tennessee** | Voluntary Pre-K | Districts may contract with private providers **rated at least three stars** | ~$85M across ~935 classrooms | **142-district business development effort**, not a state procurement |
| **Oklahoma** | Near-universal school-based four-year-old pre-K | Collaboration permitted; **contracting pathway thinly documented [unconfirmed]** | State spending ~$5,749/child | **Enrollment is falling** — see below |
| **Arizona** | No state pre-K program | — | — | **Private market carries the 3–5 load.** First Things First scholarships matter disproportionately |

### Four things worth separating out

**Oklahoma is running in reverse, and that is good news.** Four-year-old enrollment in the state's near-universal school-based pre-K fell from 39,767 in 2019–20 to **32,794 in 2024–25** — a 17.6 percent decline, from 76 percent of four-year-olds to 65 percent — while national preschool enrollment surged. NIEER's own analysis lists "children remaining in childcare centers through ages 4-5" among the causes. Roughly 3,500 four-year-olds a year have shifted out of the district pipeline since 2019. **In most universal-pre-K states the correct posture is to cede the four-year-old band; in Oklahoma it is to hold or expand it.**

**Alabama is the largest untapped opportunity in the portfolio and the number needed to size it is not published.** First Class Pre-K funds classrooms in private child care centers at $84,804 to $97,908 annually plus up to $125,000 in setup, against a 25 percent local match. Approximately 4,100 children sit on waiting lists across twenty priority counties; clearing them would take 229 additional classrooms. The named barriers are classroom space, transportation, before- and after-school care, and workforce capacity — every one of which an operator with existing licensed square footage is positioned to solve. **The classroom-by-auspice breakdown is [unconfirmed] and obtainable from ADECE Office of School Readiness at 334-224-3171.**

**The trade-off in every mixed-delivery state is the same:** the pre-K classroom brings public money but imposes a materially higher cost structure on that classroom. Alabama's First Class Pre-K requires a bachelor's-degree lead teacher and 1:10 ratios against a licensing baseline of 1:18. Georgia Pre-K ties lead teacher salaries to the K-12 schedule. Arkansas ABC requires 1:10 and one bachelor's degree per three classrooms against a 1:15 licensing ratio. **This creates a two-tier internal labor market inside the same building, and it is the primary reason pre-K classrooms and infant rooms in one center have divergent economics.**

**Georgia's HB 1123 is a direct competitive threat that has not been widely noticed.** Effective July 1, 2026, it requires public schools offering after-school programming to make it available to Pre-K students on the same basis as other students. That is precisely the before- and after-care revenue private providers hosting Georgia Pre-K rely on to make the 6.5-hour instructional day economic. With 76 Georgia centers, this warrants modelling.

## 2.7 Minimum Wage — The Structural Margin Squeeze

| State | Current | Scheduled | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arizona** | **$15.15** (Jan 1, 2026) | Annual CPI indexation, 2027 rate announced ~Sept/Oct 2026 | Constitutionally indexed under Prop 206 (2016). **No legislative off-ramp** |
| **Florida** | **$14.00** | **$15.00 on September 30, 2026**, then CPI-indexed | Voter-approved Amendment 2 (2020) |
| **Virginia** | **$12.77** | **$13.75 (Jan 1, 2027); $15.00 (Jan 1, 2028)** | HB1/SB1, signed April 2026 |
| Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Kentucky, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Texas | **$7.25 federal floor** | None scheduled | Texas preempts local ordinances |

**Three states in the footprint face legislated or indexed wage escalation. In all three, the subsidy rate benchmark is stale or frozen.**

- **Arizona:** wage +9.4 percent since 2022 against rates keyed to a 2022 survey.
- **Florida:** wage floor +25 percent from September 2022 to September 2026 ($12 to $15) against a **VPK base student allocation that has moved from $3,029 to $3,029 — zero percent** — and School Readiness rates roughly 11 to 15 percent below the 75th-percentile benchmark. This single comparison is the most powerful, fully sourced advocacy exhibit available anywhere in the portfolio.
- **Virginia:** cumulative +17.5 percent over 24 months against rates frozen since October 2022.

In the eight federal-floor states, wage pressure is market-driven rather than statutory — but market wages are the binding constraint regardless. Reported figures: Texas teacher median **$14/hour**; Georgia median **$13.35/hour** ($27,760 annually); North Carolina average **$14.20/hour**; Oklahoma **$11–12/hour**; Tennessee average annual earnings **$12,973**, with one in ten workers below the poverty line.

| **Texas** | SB 462 (2025) — children of child care workers added to subsidy waitlist priority | Direct recruitment lever against a 95,000-child waitlist |

| **Tennessee** | **PC 873 (2026)** — 16-to-18-year-olds may work in centers outside school hours, effective July 1, 2026 | New labor pool for afternoon and closing shifts. **Already in effect and largely unexploited** |
| **North Carolina** | SL 2025-36 — five years' documented experience now equals the lead teacher credential | Widens the lead teacher pool without a degree requirement |
| **Virginia** | 8VAC20-781 expands director and lead teacher pathways to career studies certificates and Virginia endorsements | Genuine hiring tailwind inside an otherwise costly rewrite |
| **Georgia** | DECAL pays employee fingerprinting costs | Explicitly conditional on continued funding — watch item |
| **Arizona** | Bright Futures AZ Scholarship, $1M for early childhood educators' own child care | Route staff to it |
| **Alabama** | T.E.A.C.H. via Alabama Partnership for Children | Credential subsidy; under-leveraged by most operators |
