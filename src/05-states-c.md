## 3.7 Oklahoma — 12 centers

> **Snapshot.** The most volatile state in the portfolio, and the volatility cuts both ways. The subsidy programme is in active retrenchment — the pandemic-era add-on ended in April, income eligibility dropped from 85 to **55 percent of state median income on July 1, 2026**, base rates have not moved since January 2023, and **131 centers have closed since April.** Against that, two structural wins for operators arrived with HB 4298 in May, and — uniquely in this portfolio — **Oklahoma's near-universal school-based pre-K is shrinking, with children staying in child care centers.**

### Licensing and regulation

Licensing sits with Oklahoma Human Services, Child Care Services, under OAC Title 340:110-3. The governing document is **Pub 14-05, "Licensing Requirements for Child Care Programs," effective November 1, 2025.**

**A technical note for compliance staff:** OKDHS's own policy-library URLs now redirect to rules.ok.gov. Oklahoma has migrated its administrative code to a new platform and any internal compliance links to the old library are broken. Separately, **the Spanish version of Pub 14-05 remains the June 2022 edition** — a compliance gap if the workforce includes Spanish-dominant staff.

**Ratios could not be retrieved from a primary source.** Appendix GG is not reproduced in the body of Pub 14-05 and the policy page redirects. A secondary source gives infants 1:4, toddlers 1:5 and preschool 1:10, **with maximum group sizes entirely [unconfirmed]. Treat these as unverified and request Appendix GG directly from Child Care Services before relying on them for staffing models.** For context, state pre-K in public schools runs a 10:1 ratio with class size capped at 20.

Directors must be 21 with a current bronze or higher Oklahoma Director's Credential and must work at least 50 percent of operating hours or 30 hours a week. **Master Teachers require a Level 4 or higher Oklahoma Professional Development Ladder certificate, with a minimum of one master teacher per 60 children of licensed capacity** — tightening to 1:30 for two- and three-star centers under legacy criteria. **This is the provision HB 4298 targets.** All teaching personnel need Entry Level Child Care Training within 90 days of hire.

Background checks include criminal history resubmitted every five years and a search of the Restricted Registry, known as Joshua's List.

**Reputational exposure is unusually high in Oklahoma.** OKDHS logs licensing visits publicly on the Child Care Locator with visit type, purpose, a count of areas in compliance, itemised non-compliances, full downloadable reports, and **a substantiated complaint history persisting for years.** Competitors and journalists can pull center-level compliance records trivially. One offsetting benefit: **accredited programmes are exempt from partial reviews** absent serious non-compliance.

**The accreditation mandate is the biggest recent regulatory change and it carries real cost.** Revised emergency rules released December 20, 2024 require **five-star facilities to obtain accreditation from one of nine approved national organisations**, with milestones on April 10, September 1 and December 31, 2025. **Failure means a star rating reduction** — and given the rate table, that is roughly a 13 percent revenue cut on infants dropping from five to four stars, or 48 percent dropping from five to three. **All costs fall on providers**, and providers testified at the January 2025 hearing that accreditation is "about a two-year process" and could force salary cuts. More than 50 providers registered to comment, largely opposed.

### Subsidy — the retrenchment sequence

Oklahoma uses **tiered reimbursement keyed directly to star level, and the tie is the steepest in the portfolio.**

**Center full-time daily rates, effective January 1, 2023 and unrevised since:**

| Age band | 1★ | 2★ | 3★ | 4★ | 5★ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–12 mo | $20.00 | $22.80 | $38.00 | $44.00 | **$50.60** |
| 13–24 mo | $20.00 | $21.80 | $33.60 | $41.80 | $48.10 |
| 25–48 mo | $17.20 | $19.60 | $31.50 | $35.90 | $41.30 |
| 49–72 mo | $17.00 | $18.50 | $22.80 | $25.20 | $29.00 |
| 73 mo–13 yr | $13.80 | $16.10 | $18.80 | $20.70 | $23.80 |

**The two-star to three-star step is the money cliff: infant care moves from $22.80 to $38.00 a day — a 67 percent increase for one star level. One star to five stars on infants is +153 percent.** Any Oklahoma portfolio strategy should be built around star advancement before volume.

Add-ons: special needs +$16 full-time / +$12 part-time daily (moderate) and +$28 / +$20 (severe); **non-traditional hours $14 a day**; child welfare case +$5 a day.

**Base rates have been frozen for roughly three and a half years through a period in which infant care costs rose 27 percent between 2023 and 2025.**

**The 2026 sequence:**

| Effective | Change |
|---|---|
| January 12, 2026 | Subsidy access **expanded** to children aged 6, 7 and 8; TANF emergency-assistance families to age 13 |
| **April 6, 2026** | **$5/day pandemic add-on ends for all recipients** |
| **July 1, 2026** | **Income eligibility reverts from 85% to 55% of state median income** |

The eligibility reversion is estimated to affect approximately **5,800 families.** The subsidy programme historically costs roughly $280 million a year against $230 million budgeted, with the gap covered by now-exhausted federal TANF reserves. Director Cartmell framed the FY2027 ask as needed to "continue to serve the 40,000 kids on the subsidy programme that we were serving at the end of September."

**One payment mechanic requires attention.** Oklahoma runs a **copay-first model: "OKDHS does NOT begin paying until services provided total more than the client co-payment."** With copays running to $473 a month, the provider bears substantial collection risk. **Combined with the July 2026 eligibility cut, expect elevated bad debt.** OKDHS is rolling out **KinderSystems** as a new provider payment system with attendance tracking; timing is **[unconfirmed]** and billing operations should pre-position.

**Whether a formal waitlist exists is [unconfirmed]** — Oklahoma rationed instead via a 2025 application and renewal freeze and the 2026 eligibility reduction.

**A recruitment tool worth using.** HB 2778 (2025) provides **no-cost child care to early childhood educators**, and the related Oklahoma Strong Start Program has awarded **$2.37 million in benefits to nearly 1,000 child care professionals.** Against wages of $11 to $12 an hour, free child care for staff is worth real money. Programme mechanics are **[unconfirmed]** and should be sourced directly.

### State pre-K — the defining Oklahoma dynamic

Oklahoma runs one of the nation's oldest near-universal four-year-old pre-K programmes, **delivered through public school districts** with no income requirement, an Early Childhood Certified Teacher, a 10:1 ratio and both full-day and half-day options. All non-charter districts offer it.

**Enrollment is falling, and that is commercially significant:**

| Metric | 2019-20 | 2024-25 |
|---|---|---|
| 4-year-olds enrolled | 39,767 | **32,794** |
| Share of 4-year-olds served | 76% (peak) | **65%** |
| 3-year-olds enrolled | 2,111 | 1,370 |

**That is a roughly 7,000-child, 17.6 percent decline in five years — while national preschool enrollment surged.** Oklahoma still ranks fifth nationally for four-year-old access and meets nine of ten NIEER quality benchmarks, but it sits 30th in state spending per child at $5,749.

**NIEER's own stated causes of the decline include, explicitly, "children remaining in childcare centers through ages 4-5,"** alongside low awareness, district enrollment friction, declining K-12 enrollment and increased homeschooling.

**The commercial read is unusual and worth stating plainly: the structural threat that school-based universal pre-K poses to private four-year-old enrollment is weakening in Oklahoma, not strengthening. Roughly 3,500 four-year-olds a year have shifted out of the district pipeline since 2019. This argues for holding or expanding four-year-old capacity rather than ceding the age band — the opposite of the correct posture in most universal-pre-K states.**

**The partnership pathway is real but thinly documented.** State law expressly permits public school early childhood programmes to operate collaboratively with other organisations, and the Department of Education names **child care centers** among documented partner types under OAC 210:15-11-2. The best-documented model is the Tulsa Head Start / Tulsa Public Schools partnership.

**Mechanics for a private operator are partly [unconfirmed].** In the collaboration model the district remains the fiscal and accountability agent, drawing state aid and contracting with the community site. **The binding constraint is the Early Childhood Certified Teacher requirement** — the teacher of record must hold Oklahoma ECE certification and is typically employed by the district and placed in the classroom, or employed by the operator at district-comparable salary. **No current policy document, application, per-pupil pass-through rate or list of active collaborative sites could be located. This is the largest single research gap in the Oklahoma profile and the second-highest priority after ratios.** Direct outreach to the Department of Education and to the specific districts of operation — Tulsa, Oklahoma City, Moore, Deer Creek, Edmond, Norman — would resolve it. The certified-teacher salary requirement is likely the deal-breaker or deal-maker.

### Reaching for the Stars

Oklahoma moved to an upgraded **five-level** system effective June 1, 2022, adding a path to accreditation for providers seeking four- or five-star ratings and criteria based on staff retention.

**A stale-source warning worth passing to any consultant.** The federal ACF and ECQuality state profiles and OKDHS's own Pub 99-39, dated November 1, 2016, all still describe the **obsolete four-level system** — One Star, One Star Plus, Two Star, Three Star. Live provider records confirm the current nomenclature. **Do not let vendors brief off the ACF profile. Current four- and five-star criteria documents could not be retrieved and are [unconfirmed].**

Also adopted: quality coaches now handle QRIS monitoring; annual criteria reviews are available on one week's notice; and the grievance first-step timeframe extended from ten to fifteen business days.

### Legislation

**HB 2778 (2025) — enacted over veto.** Authors Rep. Suzanne Schreiber (D-Tulsa) and Sen. Chuck Hall (R-Perry). Provides no-cost child care to early childhood educators. **Governor Stitt vetoed; the Legislature overrode on June 3, 2025 — House 69-17, Senate 36-9.**

*That override is the single most important political datapoint in this profile: a Republican supermajority overrode a Republican governor on a child care bill by better than four to one in the House. Child care has escaped partisan valence in Oklahoma.*

**HB 1848 (2025) failed** — a 30 percent employer tax credit capped at $30,000 per employer and $5 million annually for tax years 2026 through 2030.

**HB 4298 — approved by the Governor May 12, 2026. The most operator-relevant bill in the state.** House 95-0 and 83-2; Senate 48-0. Rep. **Daniel Pae (R-Lawton)** is the author of record at first reading; Rep. **Suzanne Schreiber (D-Tulsa)** and **Sen. Paul Rosino (R-OKC)** are the authors named in the bill's public announcement. **Note on the effective date: the Senate added an emergency clause on April 20, 2026, which in Oklahoma makes an act effective on the Governor's approval rather than the following July 1. Contemporaneous reporting described the bill as effective July 1, 2026; treat May 12, 2026 as the more likely operative date and confirm against the enrolled text before relying on either. [unconfirmed]**

1. **Master teacher ratio flexibility** — OKDHS must establish more flexible master teacher ratios while maintaining overall staff-to-child ratios. Pure margin relief, since master teachers require Level 4+ credentials and command premium wages.
2. **Differential or gap fee authority** — subject to federal approval, licensed providers may charge subsidy families **the gap between the state subsidy rate and the private-pay rate**, provided total charges to the parent, copay plus gap fee, **do not exceed 10 percent of household income.**

Forty other states already permit similar differential fees. **Quantified upside: at the frozen rates, a three-star center serving an infant collects $38.00 a day — roughly $823 a month — against Oklahoma private-pay infant rates well above that. HB 4298 permits recovery of part of that gap for the first time. And the federal rescission of the 7 percent copay cap on July 13, 2026 removed the main legal obstacle.** Confirm OKDHS has secured ACF approval and issued billing guidance before charging any subsidy family a gap fee.

**HB 1979 (2026) — enacted.** Authors Rep. Trish Ranson (D-Stillwater) and Sen. Chuck Hall, with Sen. Rosino. Creates an **Early Childhood Task Force** to examine delivery across school readiness, infant and child health, and access to care. **Preliminary report due November 2027; final recommendations November 2028.** The stated goal is to **consolidate 19 early childhood programmes currently run by five different agencies.** **Task force membership and appointing authorities are [unconfirmed] — this is a high-value seat for an industry representative and appointments have not closed.**

**HB 3705 (2026) — enacted**, raising the **Parental Choice Tax Credit** allocation cap from $250 million to $275 million. This is the private-school and homeschool credit, not a child care credit, but it is the relevant fiscal comparator: **Oklahoma has committed $275 million a year to school choice while the entire child care subsidy programme runs about $280 million and was cut.** That framing asymmetry is the strongest 2027 argument available.

**FY2027 appropriations.** The $12.82 billion budget passed April 9, 2026. **OKDHS requested roughly $70 million for child care — $57 million to restore subsidy and $11.5 million for Strong Start — and received approximately $7.5 million in additional subsidy plus $4.5 million for teacher recruitment and retention. That is roughly 17 percent of the ask.** Common education received an additional $232 million including a $2,000 teacher minimum salary raise, widening the K-12 versus child care wage gap.

**The consequence: 131 day care centers have closed statewide since April 2026.** That is a **consolidation opportunity for a well-capitalised operator**, particularly for acquiring three-star-and-above licenses in markets where subsidy-dependent independents are failing.

## 3.8 Virginia — 7 centers

> **Snapshot.** The most improved operating environment in the portfolio and the one with the clearest structural win. Virginia **eliminated its subsidy waitlist entirely in the FY2027–28 budget** and enacted a **statutory biennial re-benchmarking mechanism** that puts reimbursement rates on an escalator rather than an ad hoc appropriation. It also has the best-priced public revenue stream anywhere in this report — the Mixed Delivery Grant Program, reserved for licensed private providers, re-benchmarked at $19,441 a slot. Against that: rates have not actually risen since October 2022, the minimum wage is on a legislated climb to $15.00, and a complete licensing rewrite may have taken effect today.

### Licensing — the headline item

Licensing sits with the **Virginia Department of Education, Office of Child Care Health and Safety**, moved from the Department of Social Services in July 2021.

**8VAC20-780 is being replaced by 8VAC20-781, and the effective date requires immediate verification.** The Board of Education proposed to repeal 780 and replace it with a reorganised, more prescriptive 781; the proposed stage published December 1, 2025, comment closed January 16, 2026, and final Board approval was expected at the April 2026 meeting. **The Virginia Administrative Code now carries a Chapter 781 entry showing an effective date of August 3, 2026 — today.**

**This is [partially unconfirmed] and is the highest-priority verification item in the Virginia file.** The Agency 20 chapter index, dated July 28, 2026, still lists 780 and does not show 781, and no Register final-adoption notice could be located. The most probable reading is that 781 became effective today and the index has not caught up. **Confirm with VDOE directly.** Precedent matters: when 8VAC20-780 took effect in October 2021, VDOE granted a **90-day non-citation grace period and expired all existing variances after 90 days, requiring reapplication.** Expect an analogous transition — **and note that any variances currently held are likely to expire.**

What changes under 781:

| Area | Change |
|---|---|
| **Ratios** | Core ratios **unchanged**. School-age loosened to **max 20 per staff member (from 18)**, max group size 100 |
| **Staff qualifications** | **Loosened** — director and lead teacher pathways expanded to include career studies certificates and Virginia endorsements in child-related fields |
| Training | 3 of the 16 required annual hours must be VDOE's health and safety update course; new-staff orientation expands from 11 to 20 topics; directors must complete pre-licensure orientation within 60 days |
| **Screen time (new)** | Visual media **prohibited** birth to 16 months; max 2 hours a day thereafter unless curriculum-based |
| Safe sleep | Swaddling, weighted blankets and sleep-adaptive equipment prohibited absent physician order |
| Building | Lead risk assessments for pre-1978 buildings; CO detectors for pre-2015 buildings; potable water testing plan |
| Emergency | Lockdown drills **twice a year** (from once) |
| Records | Parents must **annually attest** to the accuracy of child records |

**The qualification loosening is a genuine hiring tailwind and should be operationalised in recruiting immediately. Everything else is added cost and documentation load** — screen-time limits, doubled lockdown drills, annual parent attestations and building assessments are the items most likely to generate citations in the first inspection cycle.

Current ratios under 8VAC20-780-350, operative until 781 lands: birth to 16 months 1:4; 16 to 24 months 1:5; two-year-olds 1:8; three years to school-age eligible **1:10**; school-age to nine 1:18; nine to twelve 1:20. **Virginia does not impose broad maximum group sizes by age; group-size caps beyond school-age are [unconfirmed].**

**Two other live compliance items.** A separate new chapter, **8VAC20-821**, took effect February 1, 2026, consolidating licensure procedures, background checks and fee requirements. And **effective January 1, 2026, providers now pay all background check fees at increased rates** — $12 for a Central Registry search, $17 for portability requests, and $63.23 for a national criminal fingerprint check for non-volunteers. **For a multi-site operator with meaningful turnover this is a real new line item at roughly $63 to $92 per non-volunteer hire.**

A **stock epinephrine** requirement was pursued as a fast-track rule but drew more than the ten objections required to kill fast-track — objectors cited recurring EpiPen cost, liability, and that "educators are not medical professionals" — and reverted to standard rulemaking. **But stock epinephrine also appears in the 8VAC20-781 package, so the requirement may arrive regardless.** Track it.

Licensed centers are inspected **at least twice annually, unannounced.** The state has 2,646 licensed center-based programmes and 1,328 licensed family child care homes. **Enforcement trend data is [unconfirmed]** and would require a data request.

### Subsidy — Child Care Subsidy Program

VDOE is the CCDF lead agency; the programme is administered operationally by VDSS through local departments of social services. FY2025 saw **$531 million in total CCDF expenditures serving more than 40,000 children**, with 53,977 children receiving subsidies at some point in the year and **47,676 slots as of December 2025.** Subsidy children are served at roughly **3,107 private early childhood centers statewide — this is overwhelmingly a private-provider-delivered programme.**

**Virginia is one of the leading states using a cost estimation model rather than a market rate survey**, developed with Prenatal to Five Fiscal Strategies. Rates reflect programme inputs — wages, standards, curriculum, quality improvement — and vary by child age, setting type and regional grouping rather than individual locality market.

**The central provider grievance: rates have not increased since October 2022.** The published maximum reimbursement schedule is dated August 2023, and multiple 2026 sources confirm rates unchanged since 2022. Four years of wage and insurance inflation have been absorbed by providers.

**Re-benchmarking is the fix in motion, and it would raise average state funding per birth-to-five slot by more than 60 percent.** Re-benchmarked cost-per-slot assumptions:

| Programme | Cost per slot |
|---|---|
| CCSP birth-to-five | $16,669 |
| CCSP school-age | $3,687 |
| **Mixed Delivery** | **$19,441** |
| VPI | $6,763 |

**Virginia pays on attendance, not enrollment.** VDOE materials state providers are reimbursed "according to actual child attendance rather than enrollment alone," and a January 2026 policy memo re-issued reminders on child-absence rules. **Virginia's compliance posture and waiver status under the 2024 federal rule is [unconfirmed]** and is a high-value advocacy opening. Two workgroup reports are the near-term documents to obtain: an attendance-and-absence-policy workgroup met in October 2025 with findings due December 2025, and an out-of-school-time rate review workgroup met June to September 2025 with a report due December 2025.

**The attendance system changed on December 1, 2025**, when **Child Care PASS**, operated by KinderSystems, replaced the legacy swipe-card system. Confirm center-level onboarding is complete at every Virginia site, since attendance capture directly determines revenue.

**Copay was cut, not raised.** Effective July 1, 2025, Virginia **reduced the maximum copay from 7 percent to 5 percent of family income** — below the former federal ceiling — and set families at or below the poverty line at a flat $5 a month per child. Eligibility runs to 85 percent SMI with entry thresholds set locality by locality.

**The waitlist was eliminated.** From roughly 3,000 in August 2024, it rose above 12,000 by January 2025 and peaked around 13,000 in June 2025, standing at 12,144 in December 2025 — and **just over 2,000 by June 2026.** The FY2027–28 budget **fully eliminates locally maintained CCSP waitlists regardless of age**, funds roughly **6,700 additional slots**, and builds in headroom for about 10 percent growth.

**One caveat worth carrying.** Advocates argue waitlist elimination overstates the win: only 26 percent of eligible under-fives are enrolled in Richmond and 13 percent in Chesterfield County. That is demand suppression, not demand absence — **and it means substantial latent demand now sits behind a cleared waitlist and a 5 percent copay cap.**

### State pre-K and Mixed Delivery

**The Virginia Preschool Initiative** serves at-risk three- and four-year-olds through school divisions **and community-based organisations**, with private providers eligible. Enrollment runs roughly 23,500 to 24,487 with a 3,351 waitlist as of December 2025; funding $116.6 million (FY25) and $115.4 million (FY26) state, against a local match requirement of $114.1 million. Re-benchmarked at $6,763 a slot.

**A structural insight worth noting: VPI persistently underspends, and unused VPI funds are repeatedly repurposed to the subsidy programme** — $7.5 million in FY25, $5.2 million in December 2024, $2.3 million in February 2025, with a 2025 budget amendment formalising the practice.

**The Mixed Delivery Grant Program is purpose-built for this business model and is the best-priced public revenue stream in the portfolio.**

- It funds publicly financed early childhood education **delivered in licensed, non-faith-based private settings** — full-day, year-round, for infants, toddlers and preschoolers.
- It is administered through the Virginia Early Childhood Foundation across **nine Ready Regions covering 82 localities**, with regional coordinators.
- It uses a **cost-of-quality payment model** since 2023 that estimates actual operating expense including educator compensation, **delivered via contracts rather than vouchers** — sustained, predictable funding supporting competitive compensation.
- Scale: **$37.4 million state general fund in FY2024 for 2,530 slots**, rising to 2,630 slots as of December 2025 with a **1,423-family waitlist**. Regional allocations ranged from $981,030 to $8,094,180.
- **Demand is roughly double funded capacity.**

**Contract-based, cost-based, priced at $19,441 a slot against CCSP's $16,669 and VPI's $6,763, explicitly reserved for licensed non-faith-based private providers, and running at roughly two times oversubscription. FY27 programme guidelines exist and should be obtained directly from VECF or the regional Mixed Delivery Coordinator — the public opportunities page returned a 404. FY27 funding totals, slot counts and per-slot rates by age are [unconfirmed].**

### VQB5

**VQB5 is mandatory for every site receiving public early childhood funds** — VPI, early childhood special education, Title I Preschool, Head Start, the **Child Care Subsidy Program**, CCAMPIS and Military Child Care Fee Assistance. **Operationally: accepting a single subsidy child at a site places that site in VQB5 with a publicly posted rating.** That is a public-reputation obligation vouchers in most states do not carry.

Three annual obligations per site: LinkB5 registration and profiles between August 15 and October 1 with curriculum reported by October 1; **two local CLASS observations per eligible classroom** (autumn and spring) conducted by the programme's own certified observers; and external CLASS observations covering approximately 75 percent of classrooms.

A points-based system totalling **800 points** produces five levels, from Distinguished (700–800) to Needs Support (100–399). **Interactions account for 700 points** — the average of all local CLASS scores times 100. **Curriculum accounts for 100 points**, awarded in full only if an approved curriculum is used in every classroom by May 31. Sites scoring 499 or below face mandatory structured improvement planning beginning autumn 2027. **Whether the current-year methodology matches the fall 2027 points system is [unconfirmed].**

**Four operational conclusions.** Two local CLASS observations per classroom per year requires certified in-house observers, and certification and recertification is a recurring budgeted expense at every site. **The score is essentially the rating** — 700 of 800 points is pure interaction quality, so there is no compliance-paperwork route to a good outcome; it is coaching, retention and ratio stability. **Curriculum is 100 free points** and is the cheapest 12.5 percent of the score available — confirm the national curriculum's approved-list status. And **mixed-age rooms are scored on the Toddler tool**, making room configuration a rating decision.

Virginia's own claim is that 99 percent of early childhood sites meet quality expectations. Read that as: a good rating is table stakes; a bad one is a serious, publicly discoverable liability.

**RecognizeB5** is a companion VDOE educator incentive programme funded at $20 million in FY25 and is a retention lever staff should be enrolled in.

## 3.9 Alabama — 5 centers

> **Snapshot.** A low-regulation, low-subsidy state with one genuinely unusual feature: **Alabama's Childcare Facility Tax Credit is written explicitly for for-profit providers, is refundable, and scales with quality rating.** Most state child care credits exclude for-profit operators. Alabama's does the opposite. It also runs the highest-quality state pre-K programme in the country — twenty consecutive years at ten of ten national benchmarks — with explicit private-center participation. **Both the credit and the political continuity behind the pre-K programme face a hard test between now and December 31, 2027.**

### Licensing

Licensing sits with the Alabama Department of Human Resources, Child Care Services Division, under Ala. Admin. Code ch. 660-5-26, **effective September 13, 2021**. **No comprehensive revision to the day care center minimum standards was adopted in 2025 or 2026** — the 2021 rewrite remains operative and no pending rulemaking for centers was identified. Licenses renew every two years.

Ratios, unchanged since July 2005: 0–18 months 1:5; 18 months to 2.5 years 1:7; 24–36 months 1:8; 2.5–4 years 1:11; **4 years to school age 1:18**; school age to 8 years 1:21; 8 and older 1:22. **Alabama does not publish separate maximum group-size caps [unconfirmed]** — which, combined with 1:18 at age four and 1:11 for two-and-a-half to four-year-olds, makes Alabama among the more permissive states in the Southeast.

Teachers need a high school diploma or GED plus at least 12 clock hours of training annually; directors need one of four pathways plus at least 20 clock hours of administration training and 4 hours of quality training annually.

**One compliance flag.** ACF identified Alabama as **non-compliant with 45 CFR 98.44, pediatric first aid pre-service training**, in the FY2025–2027 CCDF plan review. **Expect corrective action to land as a pre-service training requirement on new hires — a real onboarding cost.**

**Inspection frequency and enforcement volume trend data are not published in accessible form [unconfirmed]** and would require a records request.

**The religious exemption is the defining feature of Alabama's competitive landscape and it remains fully in force.** The 2018 Child Care Safety Act limits exemption to preschool programmes that are "an integral part of a local church ministry or a religious nonprofit elementary school," and **no repeal or narrowing was enacted in 2025 or 2026.** Exempt facilities must notify DHR, submit employee criminal history information, show proof of property, casualty and liability coverage, disclose staff qualifications and ratios to parents, **post in plain view a statement that the programme is not regulated or licensed by DHR**, and obtain annual signed parental affidavits.

**Exempt church-based centers remain price-competitive with a lower cost structure in most Alabama markets. But they cannot access the Quality STARS-gated tax credits or, in practice, subsidy tiering. That is the structural counter-advantage, and a licensed for-profit operator's Alabama strategy should lean into the incentive stack the exempt sector cannot touch — STARS 4 or 5 rating, the facility tax credit, subsidy tiering and First Class Pre-K grants — rather than competing on private-pay price.**

### Subsidy

Alabama's FFY2025–2027 CCDF State Plan was approved March 26, 2025, with delivery through four Child Care Management Agencies covering nine regions.

Eligibility runs at **60 percent of state median income** — $3,729 a month for a family of three, equivalently 180 percent of the federal poverty level — **well below the federal 85 percent SMI ceiling.** Alabama leaves substantial eligible demand unserved by design.

**Alabama pays on an enrollment basis, not attendance** — materially better for occupancy-based unit economics than the attendance states, and aligned with the now-rescinded 2024 federal rule.

**Copay is an open compliance issue.** The sliding scale runs 2 to 5 percent of gross family income, with roughly 60 percent of participating families paying no copayment. But **copays are assessed per child, not per family, with no multi-child discount — meaning a family with two or three children in care can exceed the 7 percent cap.** Alabama formally submitted a waiver to come into compliance after the start of the plan cycle. **When Alabama converts to a family-level cap, per-child copay revenue at the center gate falls for multi-child families. Model it as a receivables shift rather than a revenue loss — the state share should rise correspondingly — but timing risk is real.** The July 13, 2026 federal rescission may simply resolve the issue by removing the requirement.

**Rates are the stalest in the portfolio.** The posted schedule, "Provider Rates with QRIS Tiers," is dated **April 1, 2022**, and base rates were originally set at the **70th percentile of the 2017 market rate survey**, effective October 1, 2018. QRIS tiered add-ons above base run **+2 / +4 / +6 / +8 / +10 percent** for one through five stars.

**The 2024 Child Care Cost Study, conducted by DHR with Alabama State University, documents the gap.** Statewide 75th-percentile weekly center rates: $205 for children under two and a half, $195 for two and a half to five, $153 school-age. Documented reimbursement gaps: Huntsville centers, under two and a half — **$210 market against $168 one-star reimbursement, a $42 weekly gap**; Birmingham — $220 against $199, a $21 gap. The study concludes "almost all child care rates were higher than the reimbursement rate." The draft plan separately reports a statewide average reimbursement near **$120 a week** with 46 percent of providers charging above it and **an average $24 per-child weekly shortfall.**

**No rate increase has been announced for FY2026 or FY2027 [unconfirmed whether one is in planning].** The 2024 cost study creates the evidentiary predicate; the FY2027 Education Trust Fund gave DHR's child care line only **+$455,980**, which will not fund a meaningful across-the-board increase.

Alabama's plan does not describe a statewide waitlist, operating instead on priority enrollment categories.

### First Class Pre-K — the largest untapped opportunity

Administered by the Alabama Department of Early Childhood Education, Office of School Readiness.

- **1,521 classrooms** serving approximately **24,168 four-year-olds**, in every one of Alabama's 67 counties
- Roughly **40 percent of Alabama four-year-olds** served in 2024-25, projected to reach 47 percent under the FY2027 budget
- **Twenty consecutive years as the highest-quality state pre-K programme nationally — ten of ten NIEER quality benchmarks, unmatched**
- FY2027 funding: **$209.87 million** to the Office of School Readiness, up $12.4 million

**Per-classroom grant amounts — the numbers that matter:**

| Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| **New classroom setup**, one-time | up to **$125,000** |
| **Tiered annual funding** | **$84,804 – $97,908** by free/reduced lunch eligibility |
| Excellence funding | up to $48,300/yr on a 3-year renewal cycle |
| Per-pupil allocation, non-Head Start | up to $3,600 |

Grantees must supply at least a **25 percent local match** for tiered and new classroom grants, which may be in-kind or from alternative revenue.

**First Class Pre-K explicitly funds classrooms in public schools, private child care centers, Head Start programmes, community organisations, faith-based centers, colleges and universities, and military agencies. But the precise count of classrooms located in private child care centers is not published in any accessible source and is [unconfirmed]. This is the highest-value open data item in the Alabama file** — obtainable from ADECE Office of School Readiness at 334-224-3171, or from the annual grantee list, which names each site.

**The core trade-off:** a First Class Pre-K classroom brings $85,000 to $98,000 annually plus up to $125,000 in setup, but imposes a **bachelor's-degree lead teacher and a 1:10 ratio** on that classroom against a 1:18 licensing baseline, plus a maximum class size of 20, a minimum of 15 hours annual professional development and a 6.5-hour instructional day.

**The expansion runway is real and the barriers are ones an established operator can solve.** Sixty-four new classrooms were announced June 24-25, 2026 for the 2026-27 school year, serving roughly 1,100 additional children. **Approximately 4,100 children sit on waiting lists in 20 priority counties identified by a University of Alabama study; clearing them would require 229 additional classrooms. The named barriers are available classroom space, transportation, before- and after-school care, and workforce capacity — every one of which an operator with existing licensed square footage is positioned to solve.** NIEER recommends Alabama move toward universal pre-K.

Alabama also received a **$3.8 million Preschool Development Grant Birth through Five** award announced January 21, 2026, funding workforce supply, stability and skills, **alignment across mixed-delivery programmes**, and cross-agency analytics.

### Quality STARS

ADECE administers Quality STARS under an interagency agreement with DHR. Five levels, building-block design. **Star 1 is automatic for licensed centers unless the programme opts out.** Star 2 and above are valid for 36 months.

**Three financial ties, and they compound:** subsidy tiering at +2 to +10 percent; an annual incentive payment from DHR (**dollar amounts [unconfirmed]**); and — most importantly — **participation in Quality STARS is a hard eligibility requirement for both state tax credits, and the facility credit scales directly with star level.**

**Any Alabama center not rated at Star 4 or 5 is leaving money on the table across all three channels simultaneously.** On the facility credit alone, the delta between Star 1 at $1,000 per child and Star 5 at $2,000 per child is worth up to the full $25,000 facility cap.

### The Child Care Tax Credit Act — and its sunset

HB 358, signed May 9, 2024, effective January 1, 2025, codified at Ala. Code Title 40, Ch. 18, Article 28. **Total package value: $82.5 million over three years if fully utilised.**

**Employer Childcare Tax Credit.** Employers under 25 employees claim **100 percent** of eligible expenses; 25 or more claim **75 percent**. Cap **$600,000 per employer per year**. Eligible employees are Alabama residents, full- or part-time including independent contractors, with wages at or below $80,000, with children aged five or under. **The provider must be DHR-licensed and participating in Alabama Quality STARS.** Statewide aggregate caps: **$15 million (2025), $17.5 million (2026), $20 million (2027)**, with 25 percent reserved for small businesses or employers headquartered in targeted rural counties. Non-refundable, no carryforward. Reservations open via My Alabama Taxes on a **first-come, first-served** basis.

**Childcare Facility Tax Credit — for-profit providers only.** Credit per average monthly eligible subsidy-enrolled child aged five and under, scaled by rating:

| STARS rating | Credit per child |
|---|---|
| 5-star | **$2,000** |
| 4-star | $1,750 |
| 3-star | $1,500 |
| 2-star | $1,250 |
| 1-star | $1,000 |

Cap **$25,000 per qualifying facility per year** against a **$5 million statewide aggregate cap**, with 25 percent set aside for rural facilities and unused rural allocation moving to the general queue **after July 1**. Eligibility requires a for-profit entity, current DHR licensure, Quality STARS participation and subsidy enrollment. **The credit is refundable** — unlike the employer credit — so it converts to cash even against low Alabama tax liability. Claim no later than March 1 following the tax year.

**This is the headline provision. Most state child care credits exclude for-profit operators; Alabama's is written for them.** A larger Alabama footprint at Star 4 or 5 with meaningful subsidy census would capture materially more, but against a $5 million statewide pool on first-come, first-served reservation — **reservation timing is the whole game.** The rural set-aside releasing to the general queue after July 1 creates a **timing arbitrage** for non-rural centers.

**The critical date: all credits are available January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2027, "subject to legislative extension."**

**Uptake data for 2025 and 2026 is [unconfirmed]** — neither the Department of Revenue nor the advocacy community has published claimed-versus-cap figures. Whether the pools are oversubscribed or undersubscribed determines both reservation urgency and the political viability of a 2027 extension, and should be requested from the Department of Revenue's Tax Incentives division.

FY2027 Education Trust Fund: $10.4 to 10.5 billion, a roughly six percent increase. Early childhood lines: **Office of School Readiness $209.87 million (+$12.4 million); DHR Child Care and Afterschool $91.97 million (+$455,980); VOICES for Alabama's Children $375,000 as a new line item.** House Ways and Means Education Chair Danny Garrett set the fiscal tone for 2027: *"Those days where we had all that extra money is gone. ## 3.10 Tennessee — 5 centers

> **Snapshot.** Tennessee is simultaneously the most legislatively productive and the most fiscally contractionary state in the portfolio. The 2026 session produced two near-unanimous acts — a red tape reduction act amending local zoning law and a new dedicated child care revenue stream — while the subsidy programme cut differentials, imposed a universal copay, activated a waitlist and **flattened the return on quality investment to essentially zero.** Two of the most consequential statutes could not be retrieved and should be obtained before any Tennessee planning proceeds.

### Licensing

The Tennessee Department of Human Services regulates roughly 2,374 of the state's 4,164 licensed providers, with the Department of Education regulating the balance, largely school-based before- and after-school programmes. Rules sit at Tenn. Comp. R. & Regs. 1240-04-01.

**New licensure rules took effect November 20, 2025, with a further revision dated February 8, 2026.** TDHS ran provider training sessions in December 2025 and stated it would provide a transition period with technical assistance rather than immediate enforcement.

**The substantive content of both the November 2025 and February 2026 revisions could not be verified and is [unconfirmed] — the Secretary of State's server blocked automated retrieval. This is the single largest verification gap in the Tennessee profile** and should be closed by obtaining the rule texts directly, from the licensing consultant, or from the TDHS licensing rules guidance page.

Ratios and group sizes from the codified table — **to be treated as baseline and re-verified against the 2025–26 revisions before operational reliance**: infants 1:4 / max 8; toddlers 1:6 / max 12; two-year-olds 1:7 / max 14; three-year-olds 1:9 / max 16; **four-year-olds 1:13 / max 18**; five-year-olds 1:16 / max 20; school-age 1:20 with no maximum. **The 1:13 four-year-old and 1:16 five-year-old ratios are permissive by regional standards and are a meaningful margin advantage; the 1:4 infant ratio with a group cap of 8 is the binding constraint on infant-room economics.**

Directors need one of four qualification pathways, 36 hours of competency-based training in year one and 18 clock hours annually thereafter; caregivers need 16 hours in year one and 12 annually. **TDHS launched an Administrator Foundations Series in January 2026 aimed at new directors without four-year degrees — a useful internal promotion pathway** given the director qualification rules.

Background checks require fingerprinting within 10 calendar days and a Vulnerable Persons Registry check.

**Enforcement carries an unusual reputational mechanic.** Civil penalties run $50 for minor violations and $200 to $1,000 for major ones, escalating for background-check failures. **Probation triggers mandatory parental notification, and QRIS scores must be posted at the facility where parents can view them.** Enforcement events in Tennessee carry direct, forced enrollment-marketing consequences that they do not in states without posting mandates. **A statutory minimum inspection frequency and enforcement trend statistics are [unconfirmed].**

### Subsidy — the contraction

**Current weekly full-time rates, effective January 1, 2026.** "Top Tier" comprises the 20 highest-population or highest-income counties, including Davidson, Shelby, Knox and Hamilton.

| Age group, child care centers | Top Tier | Lower Tier |
|---|---|---|

| QRIS score | Bonus, October 2024 | **Bonus, January 2026** |

**Tennessee has substantially flattened the financial return on quality investment. If the Tennessee portfolio is heavily three-star, effective subsidy revenue is flat year over year despite a headline "rate increase."**

**Eliminated differentials, effective October 1, 2025:** the toddler differential, the child care desert and distressed county differential, and the **non-traditional hours differential.** Operators running second- and third-shift capacity or sited in rural distressed counties took an uncompensated revenue reduction.

**Copay:** families under 150 percent of poverty previously had copays waived. **From October 1, 2025, every approved family is assessed a 5 percent copay based on income**, with waivers retained only for Families First, Early Head Start partnerships, mental health and substance abuse services, and Department of Children's Services referrals. Five percent sits below the former federal 7 percent cap, so Tennessee is facially compliant — **but the 2024 rule encouraged waiving copays below 150 percent of poverty, and Tennessee moved the other way. This is both a plausible 2027 advocacy pressure point and a collections risk**, since the copay is collected through the provider relationship.

Eligibility sits at **85 percent of state median income** — the federal maximum, which is favourable. Twelve-month eligibility periods apply, and **families enrolled by August 26, 2025 remain covered through their period and may renew, so the waitlist bites only new entrants and lapsed families. Waitlist size is [unconfirmed]** and TDHS has not published it; Tennesseans for Quality Early Education is running a public "Clear The Child Care Waitlist" campaign, indicating political salience.

**Payment basis — enrollment or attendance — is [unconfirmed]** and is a material revenue-recognition question. Confirm with TDHS Child Care Fiscal at 1-800-362-8004.

**Tennessee sets base rates at the 60th percentile of market price** while federal guidance encourages the 75th; the state's own advisory commission recommended moving to a cost-estimation model.

**Child Care WAGE$ Tennessee raised its salary cap from $20 to $30 an hour**, backed by a $7.2 million state investment — a meaningful expansion of the eligible wage band.

**And one item that is directly actionable and appears under-exploited: Public Chapter 80 (2025), SB1286 by Senate Majority Leader Jack Johnson, removed the requirement that grants under the child care improvement pilot programme be provided explicitly to nonprofit organisations.** Effective March 28, 2025. **For-profit operators are now eligible for these grants.** Launch and Compliance Establishment Grants and Compliance Expansion Grants launched in December 2025 through the Community Foundation of Middle Tennessee, covering salaries, benefits, recruitment and physical space improvements. **Verify eligibility and apply.**

### State pre-K

Tennessee Voluntary Pre-K is administered by the Department of Education across approximately **935 classrooms serving roughly 18,000 four-year-olds**, funded at roughly $85 million, operating in 138 of 142 districts.

**Private provider access exists but is conditioned:** districts may contract with private child care providers, Head Start agencies and community-based organisations **rated at least three stars on the state QRIS.**

**Strategically, VPK is predominantly school-based and district-controlled — the contracting relationship runs through the local education agency, not the state, so business development is a 142-district, locally negotiated effort rather than a single state procurement.** The three-star floor is the gating credential.

**Note the tension this creates.** The state has just reduced the subsidy-side financial reward for three-star status while three-star remains the hard prerequisite for VPK contracting. **Maintaining top-tier QRIS in Tennessee is now justified primarily by VPK market access, not by subsidy bonus economics — and that is a deliberate decision to make rather than a default.**

### Quality — Report Card and Star-Quality

Tennessee runs a two-part system administered in partnership with the University of Tennessee's Social Work Office of Research and Public Service.

**Part one, the Child Care Report Card Evaluation Program, is mandatory for every licensed agency:** quarterly evaluations on a 100-point scale — **four visits annually, averaged** — assessing health and safety and teacher-child interactions across six quality areas for homes and eight for centers. **Scores must be posted at the facility where parents can view them.**

**Part two, the Star-Quality Program, is voluntary**, awarding one, two or three stars.

**The rate link is direct and formulaic, and it was cut effective January 1, 2026** as set out above. **No evidence was found of a redesign to the rating methodology itself** — the change appears to be purely to the payment schedule attached to existing scores — but a methodology update may exist within the unverified 2025–26 rule package.

### Legislation — the 2026 breakthrough

The 114th General Assembly has adjourned sine die. **Child care was a rare consensus area, with the major bills passing near-unanimously in both chambers.**

The origin is worth understanding. Two 2024 study mandates — Public Chapters 934 and 938 — directed study of the child care workforce and of **regulatory barriers imposing costs or delays on child care businesses.** Both were executed by the Tennessee Advisory Commission on Intergovernmental Relations, producing *"Improving Policies and Addressing Barriers to Grow and Support Tennessee's Child Care Industry"* in January 2025. **That report is the blueprint for essentially everything that passed in 2026 and should be read as the forward agenda.**

Its key findings: 71 percent of surveyed providers reported waitlists averaging 38 children; **eight counties have no licensed infant care and ten have only one provider**; median weekly toddler fees run $220, from $100 in Lewis County to $365 in Williamson; **average child care worker earnings are $12,973 a year, with one in ten workers below the poverty line**, and directors report losing staff to Target, Walmart, McDonald's and Chick-fil-A; one in six unemployed Tennesseans cite child care.

Its recommendations map directly onto the 2027 agenda: **align child care zoning with schools and churches**; permit home-based care by right in residential zones; eliminate special appeals for centers in appropriate zones; **eliminate minimum outdoor play area requirements exceeding state standards**; coordinate state and local fire inspections simultaneously; give child care providers **the same priority as public charter schools** for purchasing or leasing underutilised school district property; **eliminate state and local business taxes for child care businesses** — an estimated 400 to 500 providers paying an average $1,925 a year, at a cost of $350,000 to $400,000 to the state and $500,000 to localities; and move subsidy rate-setting from the 60th to the 75th percentile on a cost basis.

**The 2026 enactments:**

**Public Chapter 980 — the Tennessee Child Care Red Tape Reduction Act.** Sponsors Sen. Charlane Oliver (D) and Rep. Love. **Amends Title 13, Chapter 7 — local planning and zoning — and Title 71, Chapter 3.** Senate 32-0; House 88-0; signed May 19, 2026. **Effective May 19 and July 1, 2026.** Given the Title 13 amendment, this is the vehicle carrying TACIR's zoning and inspection-coordination recommendations. **The operative text could not be retrieved and is [unconfirmed] — obtain it. For a multi-site operator with a Tennessee development pipeline, the zoning-by-right and inspection-sequencing provisions are potentially the highest-dollar item in the state.**

*Note the politics: a Democrat led a deregulatory bill to a 32-0 / 88-0 vote. Child care is genuinely cross-partisan in Tennessee.*

Amends Titles 4, 43, 49, 57, 67 and 71 — the Title 57 (alcoholic beverages and hemp) and Title 67 (taxes) amendments indicating **a dedicated revenue stream**, reportedly drawing on hemp and vapor product revenues. **Public Chapter 873 — youth employment.** Allows a high school student **older than 16 and younger than 19 to work in a child care center outside school hours, or during school hours through a work-based learning programme.** Senate 32-0, House 94-0. **Effective July 1, 2026 — already in effect. This opens a new labor pool for afternoon and closing shifts and creates a high-school-to-career pipeline. Few operators will have moved on it.**

**Public Chapter 695 — the Child Care Hosting Safe Harbor Act**, creating liability protections for entities hosting child care services, effective July 1, 2026. Relevant to employer-sited or co-located models; Tyson Foods opened an employer child care facility in Humboldt in 2023 serving more than 100 children with a $150,000 TDHS grant.

**Public Chapter 1023 — TANF reporting.** As introduced by **Sen. Becky Massey (R)** with thirteen senators, this would have allocated a portion of the state's TANF reserve to CCDF to support Smart Steps. **As enacted, it was narrowed to a reporting requirement** mandating annual publication of TANF and CCDF balances and expenditures. **Read this carefully: the transfer authority was stripped and replaced with transparency.** That is a partial loss for the waitlist-clearing coalition — but the mandated annual report **becomes a recurring public pressure instrument that will fuel the 2027 appropriations fight.** Tennessee has historically carried a large TANF surplus; the 2021 Tennessee Opportunity Act deployed $182 million of it.

**A correction to the record.** The premise of a 2024 Tennessee franchise and excise tax credit for child care could not be substantiated and appears inaccurate. The Department of Revenue's 2024 legislative summaries contain no child care credit, and its published franchise and excise exemptions and credits page lists only the Job Tax Credit and the Qualified Production Tax Credit. **Third-party commercial sites claim a Tennessee "HB 1163" employer child care credit at 50 percent of employer contributions capped at $1 million. These contradict the Department of Revenue's own published credit list and no primary source corroborates them. Do not build a tax position on this without a written ruling.** What is real is federal §45F.

## 3.11 Kentucky — 2 centers

> **Snapshot.** The smallest state position, in the state that just completed the most consequential child care legislative cycle in its history. **HB 6 (2026)** — a Chamber-brokered, 27-sponsor omnibus — became law **without the Governor's signature**, codifying permanent free child care for child care workers, privatising administration of the state's employer-match programme, authorising a new "microcenter" category capped at ten statewide, mandating a cost-estimation rate model for the next federal plan cycle, and **freezing agency discretion over the quality rating system.** The regulatory posture is loosening. The money is not growing.

### Licensing

Licensing sits with the Cabinet for Health and Family Services, Department for Community Based Services, Division of Child Care. Note a two-agency split worth mapping for compliance escalation: a separate **Division of Regulated Child Care** sits in the Office of Inspector General.

Core regulations 922 KAR 2:090 (center licensure) and 922 KAR 2:120 (health, safety and ratios) were **both amended effective February 13, 2025**, the most recent substantive revision cycle.

Ratios and group sizes under 922 KAR 2:120 §2(2):

| Age | Ratio | Max group |
|---|---|---|
| Infant (0–12 mo) | 1:5 | 10 |
| Toddler 12–24 mo | 1:6 | 12 |
| Toddler 24–36 mo | 1:10 | 20 |
| Preschool 3–4 yr | 1:12 | 24 |
| Preschool 4–5 yr | **1:14** | 28 |
| School-age 5–7 yr | 1:15 | 30 |
| School-age 7+ (before/after school) | 1:25 | 30 |

Maximum group size applies only to Type I centers — the nonresidential category a multi-site operator occupies. **The age of the youngest child in the group sets both ratio and group size, staff members' own children count in ratio, and centers must maintain a written procedure to preserve ratios during transitions and breaks — a common citation vector.**

**Kentucky's 1:14 four-year-old ratio and 1:20 to 1:25 school-age ratios are relatively generous and materially favourable to unit economics.**

**Type I directors have eight credential pathways, running down to three years of direct experience with children in approved settings — a meaningful hiring flexibility relative to states requiring degrees.** Staff with supervisory authority need six hours of orientation within three months, nine hours of early care education training in year one, and 15 annual hours thereafter. **HB 6 adds three hours a year of special-needs training for center staff effective October 1, 2026** — budget it into FY27 training plans.

Background checks include fingerprint-based state police and FBI checks with rap-back monitoring, plus, if the applicant lived elsewhere in the prior five years, **out-of-state criminal, sex offender registry and child abuse registry checks — a real hiring-velocity drag in border markets** including Louisville, Northern Kentucky and the Tri-State. Checks must be complete **before** the person becomes a staff member, with rechecks every five years.

**Enforcement direction of travel is toward provider protection.** HB 6 imported the substance of SB 160 (Sen. Danny Carroll) as a Senate floor amendment, amending KRS 199.896 to set explicit factors governing when the Cabinet may issue a correction plan versus a directed plan versus suspension or revocation; **bar a single violation of a directed plan from triggering suspension** unless independently justified; give centers five business days to produce documentation on request; require weekly support contacts for preliminary-licensed centers; and **apply retroactively to pending enforcement actions.** HB 6 separately establishes informal dispute resolution for licensure deficiencies.

**If any Kentucky site has a pending or recent enforcement action, the retroactivity clause is directly usable and counsel should review open matters against the new standard.**

### Subsidy — CCAP

**Eligibility sits at the federal maximum of 85 percent of state median income**, having moved from 160 percent of poverty to 200 percent in January 2022 and to 85 percent SMI in July 2022. Current monthly gross thresholds effective October 1, 2025: family of two $4,862; three $6,005; four $7,149.

**A transitional provision worth modelling.** A child continues to receive assistance for six months after becoming income-ineligible — **but providers receive only 50 percent of the standard rate during that window.** That is a margin cliff on a defined subset of subsidy census, not a full loss.

**Kentucky pays on enrollment, not attendance.** 922 KAR 2:160 §17(1)(a): "Payment under the CCAP shall be made to a registered, certified, or licensed provider based on the enrollment of the child." Kentucky adopted this in the ARPA era and has retained it — **materially favourable and ahead of the 2024 federal rule.** The policy costs roughly $20 million a year.

Copays run on a **daily** chart by gross monthly income and household size, from $0 to a **$25 maximum**, set at initial application and fixed for the twelve-month eligibility period. Enrollment fees are capped at $100 per family per year for certain cases. **Explicit certification of 7 percent cap compliance could not be located and is [unconfirmed]** — the flat-dollar structure is directionally compliant but should be verified with the Division of Child Care.

Rates are published in the DCC-300 maximum payment rate chart, **set at the 80th percentile of the state market rate survey** and increased July 2021. Maintaining the 80th percentile costs roughly $40 million a year. **The current DCC-300 dollar rates could not be retrieved at any tested URL and are [unconfirmed] — this is the highest-value single missing data point for Kentucky revenue modelling.**

**HB 6 mandates a major forward change: the Cabinet must request ACF approval to use a cost-estimation model rather than a market rate survey to set payment rates for the FY2028–2030 CCDF plan**, incorporating provider type, hours and seasons of operation, child age range, geography and level of quality. **This is the most important medium-term rate-setting development in Kentucky.**

**The fiscal context is austerity.** ARPA delivered roughly $330 million a year to Kentucky child care through September 2024. The $130 million annual CCDBG replacement need decomposes as $40 million for rate maintenance at the 80th percentile, $40 million for the 85 percent SMI expansion, $20 million for enrollment-based payment and $30 million for child care worker eligibility. Caseload runs roughly 42,000 children. **No CCAP waiting list was documented [unconfirmed].**

### ECCAP — the distinctive Kentucky programme

The **Employee Child Care Assistance Partnership**, created by HB 499 (2022) and codified at KRS 199.885, splits cost three ways between employee, employer and state. The Cabinet's own example: an employer contributes $400 a month toward a $1,200 cost; **the state matches the employer's $400**; the employee pays the remaining $400. **The employer's dollar buys two dollars of tuition relief.**

The state match runs up to 100 percent for households at or below state median income, declining by 10 percent per 20-percent income increment above 100 percent, with a **50 percent floor**.

**Critically, ECCAP is a middle-income programme.** Household income must be **at or above 85 percent SMI** — below that, the employee is referred to CCAP instead, and the two are mutually exclusive.

**The provider requirement is mandatory All STARS participation.** A non-rated provider disqualifies the employer from the match. **Kentucky centers must be All STARS-enrolled to be an eligible destination for ECCAP dollars.**

Deadlines: new employer applications by **May 17 annually**; existing employer renewals by **April 2**. The fiscal year runs July 1 to June 30. **The state match is expressly not taxable compensation to the employee** — a strong selling point.

**The funding is small: $2 million in matching funds appropriated for FY2026**, first-come, first-served by contract processing order, with **25 percent reserved for small business employers** and an administrative carve-out rising to 10 percent from FY2026-27.

**Three distinct plays exist for a multi-site operator.** As an **employer**, enroll the Kentucky corporate entity and contribute toward staff child care at own centers — though most center staff fall below 85 percent SMI and route to CCAP instead, so this targets directors and regional managers. As a **provider and destination**, ensure every Kentucky site is All STARS-enrolled and market ECCAP eligibility to large local employers as a reason to steer their workforce — a differentiated enrollment funnel non-rated competitors cannot offer. And **stack with federal §45F**: employer contributions can claim the federal credit at 40 or 50 percent *while also* receiving the ECCAP state match, a genuine double benefit, with the 2025 federal law expanding qualified expenses to include intermediary service providers and technology platforms.

**HB 6 requires the Cabinet to contract with a private third-party administrator by December 1, 2026** to handle applications, eligibility verification, fund distribution and reporting, with the stated intent of making ECCAP easier for employers and providers to use — current uptake has been constrained by portal friction. **That procurement is an open, near-term influence point, and the appropriation level is the binding constraint worth lobbying.** At $2 million with a 25 percent small-business set-aside and a 10 percent administrative haircut, the programme cannot scale to a large employer's workforce.

### The child care workforce provision — now permanent

**This is Kentucky's single strongest recruitment tool, and HB 6 moved it from budget-contingent policy into statute.**

Under 922 KAR 2:160 §4(4), an employee of a licensed center working 20 or more hours a week is **automatically eligible for CCAP for their own children regardless of household income.** It operates as an income disregard rather than a separate programme, so most families qualify at the $0 copay tier.

**HB 6 §9 codifies this at new KRS 199.889**, extending it to microcenter employees. **3,200 or more child care workers and 5,600 of their children benefited in the first year**, at an annual cost of roughly $30 million.

**The qualifier "to the extent funds are available" survives codification. Codification does not appropriate, and the FY27-28 budget did not increase child care funding — verify with the Division of Child Care that the trigger has not been throttled in FY27.**

**This is worth several thousand dollars a year in effective compensation to a Kentucky teacher with young children, at zero employer cost. It should be a headline item in every Kentucky job posting and offer letter.**

### State pre-K and All STARS

The Kentucky Preschool Program, under 704 KAR 3:410, serves four-year-olds approved for free lunch — roughly 130 percent of poverty, a narrow threshold — plus all age-eligible three- and four-year-olds with disabilities regardless of income, with a space-available paid-seat pathway for others. Approximately **$170 million over the FY27-28 biennium**, but **frozen at 2019 levels through 2028** in real programme terms.

**Access is weak and declining. Kentucky serves 26 percent of four-year-olds and 8 percent of three-year-olds, with enrollment falling by roughly 900 children year over year. It meets eight of ten NIEER quality benchmarks — strong quality, poor access — and lags Georgia, West Virginia, Florida and Alabama, all of which use mixed-delivery models.**

**704 KAR 3:410 expressly authorises districts to contract with private preschool programmes**, subject to the programme being separately incorporated from any religious institution, having a nonsectarian board, maintaining separate finances and paying reasonable rent, and offering a non-religious curriculum. **A secular for-profit operator clears these tests trivially. This is a live and under-exploited channel**, and the Prichard Committee is actively campaigning for coordinated mixed-delivery expansion. Pursue district-level contracts, particularly in Louisville/Jefferson, Lexington/Fayette and Northern Kentucky.

**Kentucky All STARS** runs five levels across four domains totalling 50 points, with levels 3 and above requiring nationally standardised environment rating scale observation. **Participation is mandatory for any provider receiving public funds** — and since both CCAP and ECCAP constitute public funds, **serving any subsidy child places a center in All STARS.**

The rate tie is a **monthly per-child add-on for CCAP children: $25 to $45 a month for Type I centers serving children under three at Levels 3 to 5, and $23 to $43 for age three and above**, plus initial achievement awards of $250 to $5,000. **Moving from Level 3 to Level 5 is worth roughly $240 to $264 per child per year — meaningful but not transformative. The stronger argument for Level 5 is ECCAP eligibility and preschool contracting credibility.**

**HB 6 puts All STARS under legislative control**, requiring a comprehensive modernisation plan by **October 1, 2026**, final recommendations by December 1, 2027, and **prohibiting the Cabinet from making administrative changes without express General Assembly authorisation** except as required for federal compliance. **This is a significant governance shift: the current framework is stable through at least late 2027, and the modernisation plan process in autumn 2026 is an open comment and influence window.**

### Business climate and what to watch
