## About This Report

This report assesses the child care policy, regulatory and funding environment across the eleven states in which Child Development Schools, Inc. operates, together with the federal framework that governs all of them. It is current as of August 3, 2026.

The report covers the company's full operating footprint as published by its brands: Childcare Network in ten states, Sunrise Preschools in Arizona and Georgia, and My Small Wonders in Texas and Oklahoma — approximately 267 centers serving more than 30,000 children.

Three structural facts organize everything that follows.

**First, the federal floor beneath subsidy revenue was removed three weeks ago.** On May 12, 2026, the Administration for Children and Families published *Restoring Flexibility in the Child Care and Development Fund* (91 FR 25796), effective July 13, 2026. It rescinded the four provider-facing mandates of the March 2024 CCDF rule: the seven percent family copayment cap, enrollment-based payment, prospective payment, and the grants-and-contracts requirement for underserved populations. States may retain any of these voluntarily. None is now required. Revenue predictability in the subsidy book has become an eleven-state monitoring problem rather than a single federal guarantee.

**Second, every one of the eleven states enters a leadership transition or a new legislative term between November 2026 and March 2027.** Nine of the eleven elect a governor on November 3, 2026, and in seven of those the incumbent is term-limited. Committee assignments reset in states beginning new two-year terms. Agency heads serving at gubernatorial pleasure are in play. The relationships that produced the last two years of favorable action — Georgia's child care tax credit package, Tennessee's red tape reduction act, Kentucky's omnibus — do not automatically survive the transition.

**Third, the direction of travel diverges sharply by state.** Four states expanded materially in the last twelve months. Four contracted materially. Three held flat. A single national strategy will not work; a state-by-state posture is now a business necessity rather than a refinement.

### How to read this document

Part I sets out the federal framework. Part II provides cross-state comparison tables that permit direct portfolio-level analysis. Part III contains the eleven state profiles, ordered by center count. Part IV draws out the cross-cutting themes. Part V is a monitoring calendar for the next twelve months.

### A note on sourcing and confidence

Every factual assertion in this report is drawn from a primary or authoritative source retrieved during research conducted on August 3, 2026 — state administrative codes, legislative bill records, enacted appropriations acts, agency policy manuals, the Federal Register, and statutory reports to legislatures. Nothing time-sensitive has been drawn from general knowledge.

Where a fact could not be verified from an accessible source, it is marked **[unconfirmed]** rather than estimated. These marks are deliberate. In a document intended to support operating and advocacy decisions, a flagged gap is more useful than a confident guess, and each flagged item in this report is closable with a targeted records request or telephone call. A consolidated list appears in the Appendix.

# Part I — The Federal Landscape

## 1.1 The 2026 CCDF Rescission

This is the single most consequential development in the federal child care framework in a decade, and it runs in the opposite direction from the one the sector spent two years preparing for.

### What the 2024 rule required

The March 2024 final rule, *Improving Child Care Access, Affordability, and Stability in the CCDF* (89 FR 15366, effective April 30, 2024), imposed four provider-facing mandates on state lead agencies:

| Requirement | Provision |
|---|---|
| Family copayments capped at 7 percent of family income | 45 CFR 98.45(l)(3) |
| Payment based on authorized enrollment rather than attendance | 45 CFR 98.45(m)(2) |
| Prospective payment at the start of the service period | 45 CFR 98.45(m)(1) |
| Grants and contracts for direct services to underserved populations | 45 CFR 98.30(b)(1) |

The rule also permitted states to pay the full published rate even where it exceeded a provider's private-pay price — a margin provision of direct relevance to operators with below-market community pricing.

States demonstrated compliance through their FFY2025–2027 CCDF Plans, and states unable to comply could request temporary waivers. Waivers initially ran through August 1, 2026; in late August 2025 ACF extended the availability of additional waiver requests through August 1, 2028.

### What the 2026 rule changed

*Restoring Flexibility in the Child Care and Development Fund*, RIN 0970-AD20, was proposed January 5, 2026, finalized May 12, 2026 at 91 FR 25796, and took effect **July 13, 2026**.

| Policy | 2024 rule | In effect since July 13, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Family copayment | Hard cap at 7 percent of income | Cap rescinded. States set their own sliding scale; the only remaining test is that copays not be "a barrier to families." |
| Payment basis | Enrollment-based required | Mandate eliminated. States may use enrollment, an 85 percent attendance threshold, a five-absences-per-month standard, or a justified alternative. |
| Payment timing | Prospective required | Mandate removed. States may pay prospectively **or** within 21 calendar days of invoice receipt. |
| Supply building | Grants and contracts required for underserved areas, infants and toddlers, children with disabilities | Requirement eliminated; retained as a state option. |
| Payment agreements | — | **Retained**, at redesignated 45 CFR 98.45(m)(4), with dispute resolution cross-referenced at (m)(6). Lead agencies must still ensure providers receive payment under a written payment agreement specifying rates, payment schedules, any fees charged to providers, and the dispute resolution process. |

The rule preamble is explicit that lead agencies "continue to have the flexibility to implement the policies formerly required by the March 2024 final rule." Nothing compels states to reverse course. Nothing prevents it either.

### The rulemaking record tells the strategic story

ACF received 1,244 comments, of which 998 were unique. Of the 787 individual submissions, 276 came from child care providers. **The majority of unique comments opposed the rescissions. The majority of the seven state CCDF agencies that commented supported them.**

That split is the most useful single fact in this section. State administrators wanted the flexibility back. The pressure to unwind enrollment-based and prospective payment will therefore come from inside state agencies under fiscal stress, not from the political process — which means it will arrive through policy transmittals and state plan amendments rather than through legislation, with correspondingly less notice.

### Baseline state adoption at the moment of rescission

Before July 13, 2026, roughly 31 states and the District of Columbia had capped copays at seven percent or below; approximately 25 to 29 lead agencies had adopted enrollment-based payment; 9 to 12 had adopted prospective payment; and about 10 states used grants and contracts.

**Backsliding has already begun.** Missouri has paused prospective payments. Washington has eliminated funding for enrollment-based payment initiatives. Neither is in the company's footprint, but both are leading indicators, and both are fiscal-stress cases rather than ideological ones. **Prioritize monitoring by state fiscal condition, not political lean.**

### Immediate federal watch item

The May 2026 final rule states expressly that "forthcoming guidance will outline the process HHS will follow to terminate existing transitional and legislative waivers for rescinded requirements." **That guidance has not issued as of August 3, 2026.** It determines whether states holding waivers through August 1, 2028 simply have them voided, and how quickly. It is the highest-value single federal document to monitor.

### The TANF transfer lever

States may transfer up to 30 percent of their TANF block grant to CCDF. In FY2024 states transferred only 3.7 percent — approximately $1.38 billion nationally.

ACF has issued an information memorandum, ACF-OFA-IM-26-01, *Using TANF to Support Child Care and At-Home Parental Caregivers*, encouraging states to transfer the maximum. The full text could not be retrieved and its precise contents are **[unconfirmed]**.

This matters practically. **The TANF transfer is now an explicitly federally-encouraged, entirely state-discretionary decision that can expand subsidy dollars without a new appropriation.** Texas has already used unspent TANF for its $100 million child care scholarship expansion in 2025. Tennessee's Public Chapter 1023 (2026) now requires annual public reporting of TANF and CCDF balances — creating a recurring, official number for advocates to campaign on. In every state in the footprint this is a low-visibility ask with a real fiscal path.

The CCDBG Act was last reauthorized in 2014. Two live vehicles exist in the 119th Congress:

| | S. 2828 | H.R. 9224 |
|---|---|---|
| Title | Child Care Modernization Act of 2025 | Child Care Modernization Act of 2026 |
| Sponsor | **Sen. Deb Fischer (R-NE)** | **Rep. Ryan Mackenzie (R-PA-7)** |
| Introduced | September 17, 2025 | June 9, 2026 |
| Original cosponsors | Gillibrand (D-NY), **Collins (R-ME)**, Hickenlooper (D-CO) | McDonald Rivet (D-MI), Hinson (R-IA), Lee (D-NV) |
| Later cosponsors | Kelly (D-AZ) 6/11/26; **Britt (R-AL)** 6/16/26 | Fitzpatrick (R-PA) 7/2/26; Carbajal (D-CA) 7/2/26; Lofgren (D-CA) 7/20/26 |
| Committee | Senate HELP | House Education & Workforce; Financial Services |
| Status | **Hearings held March 19, 2026**; no markup | Referred; no markup |

**The provision that matters most to a large center operator is the shift in rate-setting from an "equal access" standard to a cost-estimation model.** States would be required to compute the actual cost of delivering care, including educator wages, benefits and operating costs, rather than surveying market prices. Market-price surveys systematically anchor to what a depressed market will bear; cost models anchor to what care costs to provide. Across a 267-center portfolio this provision is worth more than any appropriations increase currently on the table.

The bills also carry facility construction and renovation grants, reduced administrative burden on subsidy-participating providers, a strengthened requirement that states consult **providers** — not only parents — in developing CCDF plans, and support for mixed-delivery models.

**Sen. Susan Collins is simultaneously Chair of Senate Appropriations and an original cosponsor of S. 2828.** That is the highest-leverage single relationship in the federal child care landscape at present. Sen. Katie Britt, who joined in June 2026, represents a state in the footprint and has her own bipartisan child care package history with Sen. Tim Kaine.

A HELP Committee markup before the November election would be the signal that reauthorization is real.

## 1.4 Head Start

### Funding

FY2026 enacted: **$12.357 billion**, an increase of $85 million, roughly 0.7 percent. The Senate FY2026 mark had contained a 0.6 percent cost-of-living adjustment worth $77 million plus $8 million to extend the Tribal Colleges and Universities Head Start Partnership Program; **the COLA percentage in the enacted bill is [unconfirmed]**. The FY2026 process included widely reported rumors of Head Start elimination; Congress increased the program instead.

### The FY2027 restructuring proposal

The April 2026 President's Budget requests flat funding but proposes **allowing state child care licensing standards to substitute for the Head Start Program Performance Standards**, on the argument that this would "allow the program to serve more children with the same level of funding."

The strategic implication cuts both ways for a private operator. Replacing HSPPS with state licensing standards narrows the cost differential between a Head Start slot and a licensed private slot. That reduces the regulatory moat around Head Start grantees and makes partnership and delegate arrangements cheaper to operate — but it also makes Head Start grantees more directly cost-competitive with private centers. In states with permissive ratios (Georgia at 1:18 for four-year-olds, Texas at 1:18, Arizona at 1:15, Alabama at 1:18) the effect would be largest.

### The 2024 performance standards rule and its proposed rescission

*Supporting the Head Start Workforce and Consistent Quality Programming* (August 21, 2024, RIN 0970-AC63) set two compliance dates:

- **§1302.90(e) — wages: compliance by August 1, 2031.** Pay scales for all staff; education-staff wage comparability with public preschool teachers or 90 percent of public kindergarten teacher pay; salaries sufficient to cover basic living costs; wage comparability across preschool and Early Head Start programs.
- **§1302.90(f) — benefits: compliance by August 1, 2028.** Health coverage for full-time staff, paid leave, behavioral health access, facilitation of child care subsidies and loan forgiveness.

On May 12, 2026 — the same day as the CCDF rescission — ACF published a **notice of proposed rulemaking**, *Restoring Flexibility To Support Head Start Program Access* (91 FR 25842, RIN 0970-AD21), proposing to remove both subsections entirely. ACF's rationale is that the requirements exceed statutory authority under 42 U.S.C. §9848 and would, absent new appropriations, force the elimination of approximately 106,000 funded slots. The estimated effect is roughly $2.1 billion in avoided future expenditures.

**Comments closed June 11, 2026. No final rule has published as of August 3, 2026** — confirmed by a Federal Register query of all Head Start documents published on or after May 13, 2026. Until a final rule issues, the 2028 and 2031 deadlines remain technically on the books. A final rule within two to six months is the reasonable expectation, and litigation risk on it is nontrivial given the volume of opposing comment.

**For a private operator, the wage-parity mandate at §1302.90(e) was the mechanism that would have forced compensation upward at Early Head Start–Child Care Partnership sites.** Its rescission removes that cost pressure from partnership arrangements — favourable to partnership economics, but it also removes a wage floor that would have compressed the competitive labor disadvantage private centers face against public pre-K.

## 1.5 Federal Tax Provisions — The Employer Partnership Opportunity

All three relevant provisions were amended by the 2025 reconciliation law enacted July 4, 2025. **This is the strongest federal tailwind for an employer-partnership business line in two decades**, and unlike appropriations it is already law.

### Section 45F — Employer-Provided Child Care Credit

| | Prior law | Tax years beginning after Dec 31, 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Credit rate, general | 25% of qualified expenditures | **40%** |
| Credit rate, eligible small business | 25% | **50%** |
| Resource and referral rate | 10% | 10% |
| Annual cap, general | $150,000 | **$500,000**, inflation-indexed |
| Annual cap, small business | $150,000 | **$600,000**, inflation-indexed |
| Small business threshold | — | Gross receipts under approximately $32M |
| Pooled or jointly owned facilities | Not permitted | **A qualified facility may be jointly owned or operated by the taxpayer and other persons** |

Qualifying expenditures include facility construction and renovation, operating expenses, **third-party provider contracts**, and resource and referral fees, applied to up to $1.2 million of qualified operations expenditures and $1.5 million of qualifying resource and referral expenditures. Eligible entities include S corporations, C corporations, partnerships, LLCs and sole proprietors. Nonprofits and government entities are ineligible. Where the provider is primarily child-care focused, at least 30 percent of enrollees must be dependents of the contracting employer's employees.

**The pooled and joint-ownership change is the structural unlock.** Before 2026, a shared on-site or near-site center serving a consortium of mid-size employers in one office park or hospital campus did not qualify. It now does, with each employer claiming its allocable share of a 40 to 50 percent credit up to $500,000 or $600,000.

Treasury and IRS guidance implementing the amendments — particularly the **allocation methodology among joint venturers** and substantiation requirements — could not be located and is **[unconfirmed]**. Until it issues, pooled employer structures carry real structuring risk and should not be papered without tax counsel.

### Child and Dependent Care Tax Credit (IRC §21)

The maximum applicable percentage rises from 35 to 50 percent, with the phase-down restructured to deliver a roughly 15-percentage-point increase across most income ranges and a 20 percent floor above approximately $103,000 AGI ($206,000 joint). Eligible expense caps are unchanged at $3,000 for one dependent and $6,000 for two or more, and are not inflation-indexed. **Effective tax year 2026** — though one credible source places the change in tax year 2025 with a different phase-down floor, and the discrepancy should be resolved with tax counsel before use in family-facing tuition communications.

### Dependent Care Assistance Program (IRC §129)

The annual exclusion rises from $5,000 to $7,500 ($2,500 to $3,750 married filing separately), effective January 1, 2026. **This is the first increase since 1986.** It is not indexed.

### Stacking with state programs

Several states in the footprint offer state-level employer incentives that stack with federal §45F:

- **Georgia** — O.C.G.A. §48-7-40.6 provides a credit for **75 percent of employer operating cost** and **100 percent of qualified child care property** claimed at 10 percent per year over ten years, each capped at 50 percent of Georgia income tax liability. HB 136 (2025) adds a separate $1,000-first-year / $500-annually per-child employer credit against a $20 million statewide cap. Georgia is the strongest state-plus-federal stack in the portfolio.
- **Alabama** — the employer credit covers 100 percent of eligible expenses for employers under 25 employees and 75 percent above, capped at $600,000 per employer, against statewide caps rising to $20 million in 2027.
- **Kentucky** — the Employee Child Care Assistance Partnership is a **cash matching grant rather than a credit**, paid directly to the provider, and stacks with §45F. The state match is expressly not taxable compensation to the employee.
- **Virginia** — the Employee Child Care Assistance Program, enacted May 27, 2026, provides a state match for employer contributions with priority to small businesses, funded at $25 million in the FY2027–28 biennium.
- **Florida** — a corporate/multi-tax credit administered by the Department of Revenue covers employer payments **to** a facility on behalf of an employee, on a first-come, first-served basis until the cap is reached. The annual cap figure is **[unconfirmed]**.
- **Texas, Arizona, Arkansas, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Tennessee** — no state employer child care credit currently in force. Arkansas has narrower existing provisions (a 3.9-percent-of-salary credit and a sales tax refund on construction materials for employer-sponsored facilities); Oklahoma's HB 1848 and Arkansas's HB 1019 and HB 1435 all failed in 2025 and are expected to return.

**A widely circulated commercial vendor site markets a Tennessee "HB 1163" employer credit and an Arkansas "Act 1435" credit. Neither exists.** The Tennessee Department of Revenue's published franchise and excise credit list contains no child care credit, and Arkansas HB1435 died in House committee at sine die on May 5, 2025 and was never assigned an act number. If anyone in the organization has modeled either, it should be flagged.

## 1.6 Workforce, Labor and Immigration Policy

### Overtime

The 2024 Department of Labor overtime rule was vacated, and **DOL's current published guidance reflects the pre-2024 thresholds: $684 per week ($35,568 per year) for the executive, administrative and professional exemption, and $107,432 for highly compensated employees.** A Federal Register query returns no DOL rulemaking on the EAP exemption after April 2024.

Practically: assistant directors and center directors classified as exempt at salaries between $35,568 and $58,656 are not currently at federal risk. No new rulemaking is published or pending.

### Federal contractor wage floor

**Executive Order 14026, the federal contractor minimum wage, was revoked March 14, 2025 by Executive Order 14236.** DOL is no longer enforcing it or 29 CFR part 23 and is moving to rescind the regulations. This bears directly on any federal contracting, including Department of Defense child care fee-assistance arrangements.

The federal statutory minimum wage remains unchanged.

### Immigration enforcement and the workforce

Approximately **20 percent of the early care and education workforce is immigrant.** CLASP research conducted June through December 2025 — 56 immigrant parents, 74 children under six, and interviews with 67 early educators across seven states including Georgia and Texas — documents significant enrollment declines driven by family fear, including in Head Start.

Two regulatory actions bear on this directly:

- **HHS reinterpreted "federal public benefit" under PRWORA** (90 FR 31232, July 14, 2025, effective immediately), adding Head Start to the list of federal public benefits. **CCDF was already designated in the 1998 notice and remains designated.** Parallel notices issued from the Department of Education (July 11, 2025), the Department of Justice (July 16, 2025) and HUD (November 26, 2025).
- CLASP reports that the ACLU of Washington obtained preliminary injunctive relief halting implementation of the eligibility restrictions. **Case name, court, docket number and current scope are [unconfirmed].**

DHS separately published a final rule rescinding the 2022 public charge regulations on July 20, 2026, and a final rule establishing fixed periods of admission for academic students and exchange visitors on July 17, 2026.

**Operationally:** in centers with high immigrant-family census, budget for enrollment volatility that is neither price- nor capacity-driven. In centers with high immigrant staffing, I-9 and E-Verify hygiene and a documented enforcement-response protocol are now a material operating risk control rather than a compliance formality.

### Background checks

Federal requirements sit at 45 CFR §98.43 and CCDBG Act §658H — eight components comprising three in-state checks, two national checks and three interstate checks, applying to any staff member employed for compensation, including contract employees, whose activities involve care or supervision of children or who has unsupervised access. **No federal changes were made in 2025 or 2026;** the 2026 CCDF rule did not address them.

State-level changes did occur and are covered in the state profiles. Texas's SEMARC multi-agency search engine, adopted July 17, 2026, is the most operationally significant.

## 1.7 Other Federal Levers

### Child and Adult Care Food Program

Current rates are published at 91 FR 46894 (July 27, 2026), effective **July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027**:

| Meal, centers, contiguous states | Free | Reduced | Paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | **$2.54** (+$0.08) | $2.24 | $0.42 |
| Lunch / supper | **$4.76** (+$0.16) | $4.36 | $0.45 |
| Snack | **$1.30** (+$0.04) | $0.65 | $0.12 |

**Free lunch and supper rose 3.5 percent year over year — meaningfully faster than the 0.7 to 1.0 percent growth in CCDBG and Head Start.** Rates are CPI-adjusted annually each July by statute. CACFP participation optimization is one of the few federal revenue levers currently outpacing food inflation, and across 267 centers the arithmetic is not trivial.

### Department of Defense fee assistance

MilitaryChildCare.com is the central portal. **Military Child Care in Your Neighborhood** is the fee-assistance program for families who cannot access installation care, administered by Child Care Aware of America, with an expanded **MCCYN-PLUS** operating through partnerships with the Department of War. Referral logic prioritizes installation-based care where families live within roughly thirty minutes of a base.

MCCYN-PLUS reportedly allows **state QRIS ratings as an alternative to national accreditation** for provider eligibility. That is the key question for a multi-site operator near installations and it is **[unconfirmed]**; confirm directly with the CCAoA fee assistance team.

The footprint is heavily concentrated near installations — Fort Moore and Columbus, Georgia; Fort Liberty and Fayetteville, North Carolina; the Camp Lejeune area including Jacksonville, Hubert, Sneads Ferry and Swansboro; Havelock and Cherry Point; Hinesville and Fort Stewart; Pensacola, Fort Walton Beach, Crestview and Valparaiso, Florida; Warner Robins, Georgia. Congress appropriated **$100 million for military family child care assistance** in the 2025 reconciliation law.

Note also that several states exempt military-operated or DoD-certified programs from state licensing — Texas (HB 4529, 2025), North Carolina (SL 2025-36), Tennessee (HB 1663, 2026), Florida (CS/SB 738, 2025). This is a small competitive consideration near bases.

### The ARPA cliff, three years on

ARPA child care funds are fully expired. What matters now is not the cliff itself but its aftermath: **the state backfills are what is currently under pressure.** Missouri's pause on prospective payment and Washington's elimination of enrollment-based payment funding are the leading indicators of state fiscal retrenchment reaching reimbursement terms. Within the footprint, Arkansas's sustainability package expiring September 30, 2026 and Oklahoma's July 1, 2026 eligibility reduction are the same phenomenon.

## 1.9 What Is Genuinely in Play, August 2026 to Mid-2027

### Near term, high probability

1. **FY2027 appropriations against a September 30, 2026 deadline.** A continuing resolution into the lame duck is the base case. The live fight is PDG B-5 elimination — where the House and White House are aligned and the Senate is the only defense — and whether CCDBG and Head Start receive anything above $10 million.

2. **ACF guidance terminating CCDF transitional waivers.** Expressly promised in the May 2026 final rule and not yet issued. **The highest-value single item to monitor.**

3. **The Head Start wage and benefits rescission final rule.** Comments closed June 11, 2026; no final rule as of August 3, 2026.

### Medium term

4. **CCDBG reauthorization.** Bipartisan, bicameral, with an appropriations chair as cosponsor and a hearing held. The cost-estimation rate-setting provision is the one worth lobbying.

5. **Treasury and IRS guidance on the §45F amendments**, particularly allocation methodology for pooled facilities.

### The election-year window

Every current signal indicates child care is being positioned as an **affordability** issue by both parties heading into the November 2026 midterms.
