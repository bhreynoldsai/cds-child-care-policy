## 3.1 North Carolina — 77 centers

> **Snapshot.** The largest state position in the portfolio, in a state that just ended a 1,000-day stretch without a full budget. The 2026 Appropriations Act establishes **the first statewide subsidy reimbursement rate floor in the country**, effective October 1, 2026 — but the gain is concentrated in rural and small-market counties, and metro sites may capture little or nothing. Meanwhile the legislature's answer to the sector's problems has been deregulation without new money, and a directed study of **decoupling star ratings from reimbursement** is the single largest quality-related revenue risk in the portfolio.

### Licensing and regulation

The Division of Child Development and Early Education within NC DHHS regulates under 10A NCAC Chapter 09, with rulemaking by the NC Child Care Commission. DCDEE relocated its headquarters to 1915 Health Services Way, Raleigh, effective October 1, 2025.

Ratios and group sizes under 10A NCAC 09 .0713, last amended November 1, 2024:

| Age | Ratio | Max group |
|---|---|---|
| 0–12 months | 1:5 | 10 |
| 12–24 months | 1:6 | 12 |
| 2–3 years | 1:10 | 20 |
| 3–4 years | 1:15 | 25 |
| 4–5 years | 1:20 | 25 |
| 5 and older | 1:25 | 25 |

Voluntary enhanced ratios tied to star rating are available under 10A NCAC 09 .2818 (infants 1:4 / max 12; 12–24 months 1:5 / max 15; two- and three-year-olds 1:8 / max 20, with an intermediate tier at 1:9 / max 20).

**HB 412 / Session Law 2025-36** — "An Act to Make Child Care Regulatory Reforms; and to Make Building Code Revisions" — was the defining 2025 regulatory event. Primary sponsors: Reps. Dean Arp (R-Union), Donny Lambeth (R-Forsyth), Erin Paré (R-Wake) and Heather Rhyne (R-Lincoln), with Sen. Jim Burgin (R-Harnett) as conference co-chair. Its provisions:

- **A lead teacher may now supervise up to two groups**, provided at least one other individual is pursuing NC Early Childhood Credential coursework or lead-teacher job training.
- **Five years of documented experience** in licensed facilities now qualifies as equivalent to the lead teacher credential — a meaningful hiring-pipeline relaxation.
- **Maximum group sizes increased; ratios held constant** — a deliberate framing choice to blunt safety objections.
- Family child care homes received a distinct state building code classification.
- Military-certified programs were exempted from state licensing under specified conditions.

Rep. Arp's framing was explicit: "I don't see opportunity to solve child care issues by pouring significantly more money into it."

SL 2025-36 also generated a set of study directives with deadlines that have now passed and whose outputs should be obtained: a Department of Insurance group liability insurance and tort reform report (due January 1, 2026); **a DCDEE market rate study plus a plan to decouple QRIS star ratings from subsidy reimbursement (due May 1, 2026)**; and a Licensed Childcare Licensure Workgroup on conflicts among building, fire and sanitation codes.

New environment rating scale tools (ECERS-3, ITERS-3, FCCERS-3) took effect February 1, 2025, and the new Section .3200 "Pathways to the Stars" framework took effect July 1, 2025.

**Inspection frequency and enforcement trend data are [unconfirmed]** — Child Care Commission minutes and DCDEE enforcement pages were not retrievable. This is a genuine hole for a 77-center compliance risk assessment and warrants a data request. What is documented: DCDEE licensing consultants began Pathways consultation visits in October 2025, with each facility given a twelve-month window from consultation to select and transition to a pathway. **Expect elevated consultant contact through late 2026.**

### Subsidy and CCDF

North Carolina sets reimbursement at the **75th percentile of each county's local market**, differentiated by age group, facility type and star rating, with economic tier designations also factored in. **The rates currently in force took effect October 1, 2023** and are nearly three years stale.

The county-based method produces extreme geographic dispersion for identical quality. An illustrative five-star family provider serving three- to five-year-olds drew $957 a month in Buncombe County, $602 in Edgecombe and $397 in Alleghany. Alexandra Sirota of the Budget & Tax Center has put the objection plainly: the county market rate "is based more as a measure of income in a community and ability to pay and less on what it actually costs to deliver high-quality care."

**The statewide floor rate, effective October 1, 2026.** S257 establishes a floor: providers receive the higher of the 75th percentile of the 2023 Child Care Market Rate Study for their county, star level and age, **or** a statewide floor aligned with the 2021 market rate. The appropriation is **$97 million in recurring federal funds**, against a total Child Care Subsidy Program budget of approximately $593.1 million for FY2026-27. Implementation moved from July 1 to October 1, 2026 via a technical corrections bill. Roughly $27 million of the increase is directed to rural communities. **North Carolina is reportedly the first state to set a statewide floor.**

Two concrete illustrations of impact:

- Randolph County, five-star center, infant: **$867 → $1,367 per month (+$500, +58%)**
- Alleghany County, five-star family home, ages 3–5: **$407 → $900 per month (+$493, +121%)**

**The portfolio implication is unambiguous. If centers sit in metro counties — Wake, Mecklenburg, Guilford, Buncombe, Durham — the floor will likely fall below the existing county 75th-percentile rate and capture little or no increase.** The economics of this budget favour rural and small-market operators. A county-by-county model against the new tables is required before October 1.

**A sourcing caveat.** S257 is confirmed as the 2026 Appropriations Act, ratified July 2 and enacted as Session Law 2026-41. **The rate-floor provision itself, the October 1, 2026 effective date and the $97 million figure rest on contemporaneous reporting and advocacy sources rather than on retrieved session-law text — the DHHS special provisions could not be reached in the enacted act. [unconfirmed]** There is also a live figure discrepancy: EdNC reports $97 million recurring, while the Lieutenant Governor's Task Force press release of July 23, 2026 cites $101 million for "reimbursement rate improvements and statewide floor." **Confirm both the provision and the number against the Fiscal Research Division money report before modelling either.**

**Eligibility and copay.** Income eligibility runs to 200 percent FPL for birth to age five and 133 percent FPL for school-age children five to twelve. **The family copay runs up to 10 percent of gross family income** — North Carolina's long-standing percent-of-income structure, above the former federal seven percent benchmark and now unambiguously permissible following the July 13, 2026 rescission.

**Waitlist:** 2,164 children in July 2024, rising to **15,512 by December 2025** — roughly a sevenfold increase. A separate count put 18,556 children aged zero to five on the waitlist in September 2025 against 55,220 served. Subsidy enrollment is down about 15 percent since 2019, and roughly 60 percent of licensed programs participate.

**Supply contraction.** 5,160 licensed programs in January 2026, down from 5,921 in July 2019 — a 13 percent net loss, projected below 5,000 in 2027. **280 licensed providers closed between January and October 2025**; in August 2025 alone, 47 centers closed against 22 openings. Family child care homes account for 97 percent of the net loss. Federal stabilization grants concluded in March 2025 with **no direct state successor compensation programme**; the rate floor is the de facto successor, arriving roughly eighteen months after the cliff.

**Post-rescission payment basis is [unconfirmed] and is the highest-magnitude open risk in this profile.** North Carolina's fiscal posture and its stated legislative interest in "cost reductions without new funding" create real risk of reversion toward attendance-based reimbursement. The state's FFY2025–2027 CCDF Plan, submitted November 2024, predates the rollback and will likely be amended.

### State pre-K

NC Pre-K is administered by DCDEE, not the Department of Public Instruction. Eligibility requires age four by August 31 and family income at or below 75 percent of state median income, with up to 20 percent of slots available above the income limit for developmental delay, disability, chronic health condition or limited English proficiency. Delivery is mixed — public schools, Head Start, and both nonprofit and private for-profit child care centers.

Most recent published data (2023–24): **27,304 four-year-olds enrolled, 22 percent of the state's four-year-old population**; 57 percent of eligible children enrolled. State spending **$115.3 million**, plus federal TANF of $66.8 million. **State spending per child $7,117** against a national state average of $7,888.

**The FY2026-27 budget added no new NC Pre-K funding** and imposed a dyslexia screening requirement on all NC Pre-K students with mandated staff training — an unfunded compliance cost. Governor Stein had recommended $11 million to raise NC Pre-K reimbursement rates by six percent across all settings; it was not enacted. Providers have publicly warned that rates are too low to sustain participation. **Current NC Pre-K per-slot payment rates by site type for 2026-27 are [unconfirmed]**; the latest published rate guidance located dates to 2015.

### Quality rating

North Carolina pioneered QRIS nationally in 1999. Every licensed center carries a one- to five-star rated license, and **market rate tables are published by county and star level**, so a five-star licence commands materially higher reimbursement than a three-star in the same county. Stars also gate access to the voluntary enhanced ratios.

**"Pathways to the Stars," codified at Section .3200, took effect July 1, 2025**, replacing the prior points approach with three pathways: Program Assessment (ECERS-3 / ITERS-3 / FCCERS-3), Classroom and Instructional Quality, and **Accreditation and Head Start**.

**Pathway 3 is a potentially significant efficiency for a multi-state operator already carrying national accreditation** — it may permit star maintenance without the full environment rating scale assessment burden. This warrants a direct DCDEE conversation, and an inventory of which centers have already been consulted and how much of their twelve-month transition window remains.

**The decoupling threat.** SL 2025-36 directed DCDEE to submit, by May 1, 2026, a plan to separate the quality rating system from subsidy reimbursement rates. Separately, DCDEE issued RFA #007 on November 7, 2025 for a "QRIS Support and Supplement Study," with UNC's FPG Child Development Institute in the bidder pipeline. **Whether the decoupling plan was delivered, and its content, is [unconfirmed].** If decoupling advances, an operator that invested in four- and five-star quality loses its rate premium across 77 centers. This is the highest-value single retrieval in the North Carolina file and the most likely 2027 legislative target.

### 2027 outlook

The 2027 long session convenes in January and writes the FY2027-29 biennial budget — the first full biennium cycle after a two-year budget failure, and the first real test of whether the rate floor holds and grows.

Current composition is Senate 30R–20D and House 71R–49D. **House Republicans are one seat short of a veto-proof majority.** All 170 legislative seats are on the November 3, 2026 ballot, with a marquee U.S. Senate race (Whatley v. Cooper) driving turnout. Governor Stein is not up.

- If Republicans regain a House supermajority, the deregulatory agenda — QRIS decoupling, further ratio and group-size flexibility, code streamlining — proceeds over Stein's objection.
- If Democrats hold or gain, Stein's veto remains binding and funding-side proposals gain leverage.

Teed up for 2027: QRIS decoupling; the Licensed Childcare Licensure Workgroup's code-conflict report and a likely streamlining bill; **liability insurance**, where the Department of Insurance workgroup report was due January 1, 2026 and the budget added $350,000 for a feasibility study; child care for child care workers (HB 800 / SB 593 concepts); rate floor phase two, being the remaining $279 million of the $380 million plan; and Governor Stein's proposed refundable Child and Dependent Care Tax Credit ($55 million) and Working Families Tax Credit at ten percent of the federal EITC ($240 million), neither of which appears in the enacted budget.

### Business climate

Average child care teacher wage **$14.20/hour** (2024) across a workforce of roughly 42,000. Only 49 percent of surveyed centers offer fully or partially subsidised health insurance. **North Carolina has no enacted state child and dependent care tax credit.**

SL 2025-36 created a family child care classification in the state building code and directed the Licensed Childcare Licensure Workgroup to examine conflicts among building, fire and sanitation codes. **That workgroup report is the most likely vehicle for center-level code relief and is an open lobbying lane for an operator with a construction and conversion pipeline.** No statewide zoning preemption for child care appears to exist.

Hurricane Helene damaged at least 55 licensed programs; $700 million sits in the budget for recovery. The Child Care Expansion and Access Grants programme — 200 grants of up to $125,000 from a $20 million appropriation — closed to applications in June 2023 with no identified successor capital programme.

## 3.2 Georgia — 76 centers

> **Snapshot.** The second-largest state position, and the state with the sharpest internal contradiction in the portfolio. Georgia Pre-K — lottery-funded, roughly half privately delivered, meeting ten of ten national quality benchmarks — is the strongest and most stable public revenue line available anywhere in the footprint, and it keeps getting better funded. Simultaneously, CAPS has contracted to **the lowest initial subsidy eligibility threshold in the country**. And the architect of Georgia's entire child care tax agenda lost his primary in June.

### Licensing and regulation

Bright from the Start: Georgia Department of Early Care and Learning (DECAL) is **both the licensing agency and the CCDF lead agency** — an unusual single-agency consolidation that also runs Pre-K, CACFP, Head Start Collaboration and the Criminal Records Unit. In a multi-state portfolio this is a genuine advantage: one relationship covers licensing, subsidy and pre-K.

Child Care Learning Centers are governed by Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. Chapter 591-1-1, consolidated version effective July 1, 2025. An amendment package effective June 4, 2025 updated pediatric first aid and CPR requirements. **No new proposed rule package was posted as of early August 2026.**

Ratios and group sizes under Rule 591-1-1-.32:

| Age | Ratio | Max group |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1 year / under 18 months non-walking | 1:6 | 12 |
| 1 year (walking) | 1:8 | 16 |
| 2 years | 1:10 | 20 |
| 3 years | 1:15 | 30 |
| 4 years | **1:18** | 36 |
| 5 years | 1:20 | 40 |
| 6 and older | 1:25 | 50 |

Group-size caps do not apply to outdoor playground time, special center activities of two hours or less, or centers licensed for eighteen or fewer children — but ratios apply at all times. **Georgia's 1:15 at age three and 1:18 at age four are among the most permissive in the country and represent a material margin advantage** relative to neighbouring states. Note the separate and much tighter Georgia Pre-K standard of 1:10 with a maximum class size of 20.

Directors must complete a 40-hour DECAL-approved director's training course. All direct-care staff need pediatric CPR on a biennial cycle and pediatric first aid on a triennial cycle, obtained within the first 45 days of employment. **Annual continuing-education hour requirements are [unconfirmed]** and should be read directly from 591-1-1-.14/.15.

**Background checks — the portability provision is operationally valuable.** Georgia Child Care Background Records requires fingerprint-based checks for every person working at a licensed center, with two determinations: a National Records Check permitting supervised work while out-of-state records pend, and a Comprehensive Records Check required for unsupervised access. Renewal every five years, or after the first year on a facility change. **Determination letters less than one year old can be electronically ported to a different facility without a new check** — materially useful for moving staff between 76 centers. **DECAL currently pays the fingerprinting cost** for child care employees "for the foreseeable future unless funding becomes no longer available" — an explicitly conditional state subsidy of a compliance cost and therefore a watch item.

**DECAL enforcement volumes and trend direction for FY2024–FY2026 are [unconfirmed]** — the enforcement actions page was inaccessible. This is the largest data gap in the Georgia licensing section. An open-records request for enforcement action counts by type, together with a pull of each center's own inspection history from the DECAL portal, would close it.

DECAL launched **DECAL FAST** — Family Access to Services & Tools — on July 10, 2026, a consolidated family portal with an AI navigation assistant, Spanish-language content and mobile-first design. Provider-side implications were not detailed, but it is worth confirming whether the new portal changes how centers appear in state-facing search and referral, and whether Quality Rated status is now surfaced more prominently to families.

### Subsidy and CCDF

**Georgia has materially contracted its subsidy programme since 2024, and the federal floor beneath it was removed in July 2026.**

State funding for Childcare and Parent Services was approximately **$77 million in FY2026 and $77.5 million in FY2027** — growth of 25 percent over eight years against 31 percent inflation, a real-terms cut, while Pre-K grew 57 percent. Georgia lost more than $170 million in federal COVID-era relief in September 2024; GEEARS puts the resulting gap at roughly $188 million.

**The eligibility change is the defining fact.** DECAL cut initial income eligibility from 50 percent of state median income ($52,947 for a family of four) to **30 percent of SMI ($31,768 for a family of four)** — described by the Georgia Budget and Policy Institute as the lowest initial eligibility threshold in the country. Families must also participate in at least 24 hours a week of approved work or school activity and fall within a DECAL-designated priority group. Continued eligibility at redetermination runs to the federal ceiling of 85 percent SMI.

Note a live conflict in the record: GEEARS' CAPS materials still state 50 percent SMI. Treat 30 percent as operative — GBPI is the more recent and more specific source — but verify with DECAL. CAPS Appendix A carries an effective date of March 2, 2026 and should be pulled directly.

**Enrollment fell from a peak of roughly 72,000 children in 2023 to roughly 51,000.** Approximately 24,000 children lost scholarships when pandemic funding ended in 2024. Only about seven percent of income-eligible children are served. Recent additions are small: FY2026 added $4 million for 500 scholarships; FY2027 redirected $9.7 million in freed federal funds to serve 1,288 more children. **Formal waitlist status is [unconfirmed]** — Georgia manages access through the eligibility threshold and priority groups rather than a published queue.

**Rate methodology.** Rates are set at the **60th percentile** of the most recent market rate survey — one of the lowest benchmarks in the portfolio. The current basis is a 2024 analysis by Care Solutions, Inc. of 2023 state administrative rate data, not a fresh provider survey. Rates vary by zone, age group and Quality Rated star level.

**A new market study is underway in 2026 with Georgia Policy Labs at Georgia State University**, in two parts: a Child Care Market Rate Survey covering all licensed providers, and a **Child Care Cost Survey** of selected Quality Rated CAPS providers — the cost-estimation methodology the 2024 federal rule encouraged. **This is the single most consequential pending item for CAPS rate levels.** Cost-based methodologies systematically produce higher rate benchmarks than price surveys. Georgia centers should be in the sample and should submit complete cost data. Contact: ChildCareMarketStudy@decal.ga.gov.

Family copay runs on a sliding scale keyed to federal poverty level bands, one fee per family divided across children: no fee at or below ten percent FPL; three percent of annual income at 10–50 percent; five percent at 50–100 percent; seven percent above 100 percent. **These figures come from a secondary source and should be verified against CAPS Policy Manual §00-9.**

**Payment basis is [unconfirmed] and is the highest-value open question in the Georgia file.** At least one industry source states Georgia operates an attendance-based model with mandatory date-stamped digital attendance documentation. Treat as plausible, not confirmed, and obtain the answer from CAPS Policy Manual §00-12 or the CAPS provider agreement. Whatever the current practice, **there is now no federal barrier to Georgia retaining or reverting to attendance-based, in-arrears payment** — a direct cash-flow and bad-debt exposure across 76 centers.

### Georgia Pre-K

This is the most attractive and most stable revenue line in Georgia for a private operator, and it has been getting steadily better funded.

| | FY2026 | FY2027 enacted |
|---|---|---|
| DECAL total state funds | ~$640M | **$671.5M** (+$31.1M) |
| Georgia Pre-K | $563M | **$594M** |
| CAPS | ~$77M | $77.5M |

FY2027 added roughly $30.7 million to Pre-K: **$12 million** for year three of the four-year phase-in reducing class size from 22 to 20; **$9.7 million** for extended-day expansion for priority groups and lottery-funded before- and after-school scholarships; **$4 million** for five days of paid leave for Pre-K lead and assistant teachers, a new benefit; **$5.9 million** for teacher health-benefit cost increases; and **$708,000** for Rising Pre-K Summer Transition Program expansion.

**Class size and ratio.** DECAL's 2026-2027 Pre-K Application Overview states the maximum class size for all Georgia Pre-K classrooms is **20 students**, with inclusion classrooms capped at 18, staffed by one lead teacher and one assistant. NIEER records the standard as maximum 20 with a 1:10 ratio for 2024-25, up from maximum 22 and 1:11 in 2023-24. **The budget phase-in is funding the revenue backfill for classrooms that lost two slots, not a further rule change.**

Program requirements for 2026-2027: 180 instructional days plus 10 professional development days; a 6.5-hour instructional day; DECAL-approved curriculum; **a $30,000 startup grant per new classroom** for materials and equipment. Private centers qualify if DECAL-licensed with licensed space for at least 20 children and a playground accommodating 20. The application window ran February 23 to March 18, 2026, and the rate chart publishes only after gubernatorial budget approval.

**Enrollment and quality.** 2024-25: **70,572 children, 53 percent of Georgia four-year-olds**; $538.4 million total state spending; **$7,630 per child**, a 22 percent budget increase year over year. Georgia met **ten of ten NIEER quality benchmarks in 2024-25**, up from eight of ten — a credential worth deploying in state-level advocacy.

**Teacher compensation is a wage-floor pull on the rest of the building.** Lead teacher salaries are tied to the State Board of Education K-12 salary schedule. 2024-25 delivered a 27.5 percent assistant teacher salary increase and funding for 100 new classrooms; FY2026 included $18.8 million for one-time $2,000 raises, of which $17.2 million went to Pre-K teachers. **Georgia Pre-K salary parity is the primary reason Pre-K classrooms and infant rooms in the same building have divergent labor economics.**

**Private provider share.** DECAL states participation is voluntary for both public schools and private child development centers. GBPI survey research found enrollment roughly evenly split between public schools and private providers across FY2012–FY2019. A current precise split is **[unconfirmed]**. Use "approximately half" and request the current figure from DECAL's Pre-K division. **This is the core political fact for the sector's standing in Georgia: no other state pre-K program of this scale is so dependent on private and community providers.**

**Expansion pressure.** GEEARS is pushing a pilot to serve three-year-olds and points to **$1.7 billion in unrestricted lottery reserves** as the source. The 2026 vehicle, HB 1146, died in House Education but the reserve balance makes this a recurring ask — and a three-year-old pilot delivered through the existing roughly-half-private network would be a direct new revenue line.

### Quality Rated

Georgia's QRIS is a voluntary one-, two- or three-star system, but participation is functionally mandatory: **"as of December 31, 2021, all child care providers must be Quality Rated (or in Provisional or Probationary Status) to receive CAPS funding."** Scoring runs to 45 points — Structural Quality (portfolio, 0–15), Process Quality (environment rating scale observation, 0–30), and an accreditation bonus of up to 4. Ratings are valid three years.

Financial incentives include an annual Quality Rated Workforce Bonus, Commitment to Quality payments, and customisable bonus packages covering credentialing, equipment, curriculum and professional development, administered through decalqrpayments.com. **Current dollar amounts are [unconfirmed].**

**A proposed Quality Rated Framework revision was discussed in May 2024 and its status is [unconfirmed]** — a priority follow-up, because a re-weighted framework can move star levels and therefore tiered reimbursement across an entire portfolio at once.

### 2025–2026 legislation

**HB 136 (2025) — Act 182**, the child care tax credit package. Lead sponsor Rep. Mark Newton (R-Augusta), carried as a priority of Lt. Gov. Burt Jones and derived from the Senate Study Committee on Access to Affordable Childcare. House 170–2; Senate 50–0; signed May 13, 2025; effective July 1, 2025.

| Credit | Terms | Effective |
|---|---|---|
| Child & Dependent Care Expense credit | Raised from **30% to 50% of the federal credit** | Tax years from January 1, 2025 |
| Georgia Child Tax Credit (new) | **$250 per qualifying child under age 6** | Tax years from January 1, 2026 |
| **Employer Child Care Credit (new)** | **$1,000 per child in year one, $500/child annually thereafter**; requires employer payments of ≥$1,000/yr per employee to a **licensed** facility for children under 6; **$20M annual statewide cap** | January 1, 2026; **sunsets December 31, 2030** |

All three are nonrefundable, capped at the taxpayer's income tax liability, with no carryforward.

**Georgia's pre-existing employer credit at O.C.G.A. §48-7-40.6 remains in force and is far larger in value:** 75 percent of the employer's cost of operation less employee payments, capped at 50 percent of annual income tax liability with a five-year carryforward; and 100 percent of qualified child care property claimed at ten percent a year over ten years, capped at 50 percent of liability with a three-year carryforward. The facility must be DECAL-licensed and **at least 95 percent of users must be children of participating employees** — the binding constraint on any consortium structure.

**The go-to-market read: §48-7-40.6 is the on-site and near-site consortium play; HB 136 is the tuition-benefit play.** The HB 136 credit is small and nonrefundable, but its low threshold of $1,000 a year per employee makes it a workable door-opener with mid-size Georgia employers, and the "licensed facility" requirement means every licensed center qualifies as recipient. The 2030 sunset supplies urgency.

**2026 session — passed:**

- **HB 1123 (Act 393)** — Pre-K access to school after-school programs. Sponsors Rep. Jan Jones and others. Requires public schools offering after-school programming to make it available to Pre-K students on the same basis as other students. House 162–0; Senate 46–0; signed May 5, 2026; **effective July 1, 2026.** **This is a direct competitive threat to the wraparound revenue private Georgia Pre-K hosts depend on. Model it.**
- **SB 589 (Act 633)** — Pre-K and kindergarten age eligibility. Lets parents elect to enroll a child who has attained age four **or five** in voluntary Pre-K rather than kindergarten. Senate 54–0; House 156–3; signed May 12, 2026; effective July 1, 2026, applicable to the 2026-2027 school year. **This legally enlarges the Pre-K-eligible pool by adding opt-in five-year-olds — favourable for private Pre-K sites with capacity.**
- **HB 1193** — early literacy coaches at all 1,850 public elementary schools ($70 million school-based, $18.5 million regional), with Pre-K collaboration requirements attached.

**2026 session — died, all likely to return in 2027:**

- **SB 554** — child care facility capital grant program, directing DECAL to establish grants for facility repairs, renovations and expansions. Sponsors Sens. Chuck Payne, Jason Anavitarte, Billy Hickman, Randy Robertson (R), Sonya Halpern and Tonya Anderson (D). Died in Senate Education and Youth. A GEEARS priority. **The single most directly favourable pending bill for a capital-intensive operator, with bipartisan sponsorship and a Republican Senate Education member as lead.**
- **HB 1281** — mandatory liability insurance for child care learning centers with specified minimums and proof of filing. Six Republican sponsors. Died in House Education. **A cost-of-compliance risk, but consider that a well-calibrated mandate is often net-favourable to a large, well-capitalised operator against undercapitalised independents. Engaging constructively on limits may be better than opposing.**
- **HB 1146, the Georgia Ready to Learn Act** — a pilot expanding Georgia Pre-K to three-year-olds. Died in House Education. A GEEARS priority and a direct new revenue line if enacted.

### 2027 outlook

**The 2027 session convenes in January and begins a new two-year term — every bill that died in 2025-26 is dead and must be reintroduced, all committee assignments reset, and a new Governor and Lieutenant Governor take office.** Combined with executive-branch turnover, 2027 is a genuine reset year.

Governor Kemp is term-limited. In the May 19, 2026 primaries, **Keisha Lance Bottoms won the Democratic nomination outright with 57.7 percent**. On the Republican side no candidate reached a majority — Burt Jones 38.0 percent, Rick Jackson 33.9 percent, Brad Raffensperger 14.5 percent, Chris Carr 11.4 percent — and **Rick Jackson defeated Lt. Gov. Burt Jones in the June 16 runoff, 52.7 to 47.3 percent.** The general election is November 3, 2026, with a December 1 runoff if no majority. Rated toss-up to tilt Republican.

**This is the most consequential political fact in the Georgia file.** Burt Jones — the architect and chief patron of Georgia's entire child care tax credit agenda — leaves office in January 2027, and the 2024 Senate Study Committee that produced HB 136 has no visible 2026 successor. **The child care agenda in Georgia needs a new Republican patron.**

On candidate positions: **Rick Jackson (R)** is a healthcare staffing executive who grew up in foster care across five homes and led passage of the Fostering Success Act providing tuition-free public college for foster youth. His platform centers on cutting the state income tax in half within four years and eliminating it within eight, freezing property taxes, term limits, AI-driven program audits and work requirements for public assistance. **No explicit child care or early learning plank was found [unconfirmed].** Two inferences: his foster-care biography is a plausible opening for a child-wellbeing framing; and **an aggressive income-tax elimination trajectory is a structural threat both to CAPS, which is general-fund financed, and to the value of nonrefundable credits like HB 136's** — if there is no income tax, an income tax credit is worthless. Georgia Pre-K, being lottery-financed, is insulated. **Keisha Lance Bottoms (D)** — former Atlanta mayor and Biden White House senior adviser — has no retrievable specific early learning platform **[unconfirmed]**.

Issues teed up for 2027: SB 554 capital grants redux; Pre-K expansion to three-year-olds against the $1.7 billion lottery reserve; CAPS restoration to 72,000 children and reversal of the 30 percent SMI threshold; year four of the Pre-K class-size phase-in in the FY2028 budget; a liability insurance mandate refile; **and Georgia's response to the July 2026 federal CCDF deregulation.**

### Political and agency map

**DECAL Commissioner Amy M. Jacobs** was appointed by Governor Deal in June 2014 and retained under Kemp — a twelve-year tenure, extraordinary stability for a state agency head. Her background is the Governor's Office of Planning and Budget, where she directed the Education and General Government divisions. **That background means DECAL is unusually fluent in budget process — an advantage when advocating for appropriations rather than statutory change.** She remained in post as of July 2026. **A new Governor takes office in January 2027; commissioner retention is a real open question and a top monitoring item.**

Committee chairs could not be retrieved and are **[unconfirmed]**; all assignments reset in January 2027 in any case. What is confirmed: **Senate Education and Youth** received SB 554 and SB 589 and is the primary Senate venue; **Senate Children and Families** received HB 1123; **House Education** killed both HB 1281 and HB 1146 and is the key House chokepoint. Legislators to know from the bill record: Speaker Pro Tem **Jan Jones** (HB 1123 lead); **Sen. Chuck Payne** (SB 554); **Sen. Matt Brass** (SB 589); **Rep. Mark Newton** (HB 136); **Sens. Jason Anavitarte and Billy Hickman**, on both SB 554 and SB 589; **Rep. Chris Erwin**, central House education actor; and **Rep. Joseph Gullett**, who co-sponsored both the Pre-K-for-threes bill and the insurance mandate.

**GEEARS — the Georgia Early Education Alliance for Ready Students — is the dominant early-learning advocacy organisation and the natural coalition partner.** Its 2026 agenda includes restoring CAPS to 72,000 children, **dedicated capital improvement grants for child care facilities**, prioritising early educators' own children for CAPS, dedicated infant-toddler funding, and a lottery-funded Pre-K pilot for three-year-olds. Also active: Voices for Georgia's Children; the Georgia Budget and Policy Institute, whose annual Education Primer and DECAL budget overviews are the de facto reference documents legislators use.

**The Georgia Chamber of Commerce runs a Center for Workforce & Talent Preparedness and the Georgia|2050 strategic plan, but no child care–specific program, coalition or policy position is visible on its site [unconfirmed].** Given that HB 136 was framed as a workforce measure the Chamber almost certainly supported it, but child care is not a headline Chamber brand in Georgia the way it is in Kentucky or North Carolina. **That is an opening: a large operator could help stand up a Georgia business-and-child-care coalition rather than joining one.**

### Business climate

Georgia child care worker median hourly wage **$13.35**, annual median $27,760 (2023). Infant-toddler teachers earn less than Pre-K teachers, who benefit from lottery-funded pay schedules — **the state has effectively created a two-tier wage structure inside the same buildings.**

Roughly 4,500 licensed providers offer approximately 368,000 licensed slots against approximately 1,115,000 children under 13 with all available parents working. **44 percent of Georgians live in a child care desert.** Georgia's child care shortfall costs the state economy at least $1.75 billion annually — the standard business-case statistic in Georgia advocacy.

**No state-level zoning preemption, by-right siting provision or child care zoning reform was identified in the 2025 or 2026 sessions [unconfirmed].** Georgia child care siting appears entirely local. This is an unaddressed policy space and a plausible 2027 ask, and SB 554's capital-grant framing is a natural vehicle to attach it to. Property tax treatment of child care facilities is likewise **[unconfirmed]**.

### What to watch

1. **The DECAL / Georgia Policy Labs market rate and cost study now in progress.** This resets the CAPS rate benchmark and is the highest-dollar-impact item in the state. Confirm Georgia centers are in the Child Care Cost Survey sample and submit complete cost data.
2. **Georgia's response to the July 13, 2026 federal deregulation.** Get a definitive answer from DECAL on enrollment versus attendance and on payment timing, then model the cash-flow delta of a reversion to attendance-based, in-arrears payment across 76 centers.
3. **November 3, 2026 and the January 2027 transition.** Watch three things: retention of Commissioner Jacobs; the identity of the new Lieutenant Governor, who controls Senate referral; and whether income tax elimination under a Jackson administration erodes both CAPS general-fund capacity and the value of nonrefundable HB 136 credits.
4. **SB 554 refile — child care facility capital grants.** Engage Sen. Chuck Payne's office during the interim, offer facility cost data, and consider attaching a zoning or by-right provision.
5. **HB 1123 implementation and the FY2028 Pre-K budget.** Model the wraparound revenue exposure at private Georgia Pre-K sites, and watch for year four of the class-size phase-in and annualisation of the extended-day and paid-leave lines.
