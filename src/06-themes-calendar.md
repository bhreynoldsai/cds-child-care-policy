# Part IV — Cross-Cutting Themes

Eleven state profiles produce a great deal of detail. Six patterns run across all of them, and they are what a portfolio-level view adds to a state-level one.

## 4.1 The federal floor is gone and the consequences are now local

For two years the sector planned against a single federal standard. That standard disappeared on July 13, 2026, and what replaced it is eleven separate policy environments in which the same four questions — copay ceiling, payment basis, payment timing, and whether direct-service contracts exist — now have eleven independent answers that can change without legislation.

The practical consequences are three.

**Monitoring cost has risen and monitoring cadence has to change.** State agencies signalled in the rulemaking record that they wanted this flexibility. Changes will therefore arrive through policy transmittals, provider agreements and CCDF state plan amendments rather than through bills — instruments with far shorter notice periods and no committee hearings. Texas's Chapter 809 package and Florida's Rule 6M-4.500 amendment are both examples of consequential change moving through rulemaking while nothing appeared on a legislative calendar.

**Fiscal condition predicts behaviour better than political lean.** The two documented reversions to date — Missouri pausing prospective payment, Washington defunding enrollment-based payment — are budget decisions, not ideological ones. Within the footprint, the states with the sharpest fiscal pressure are Arkansas (a cliff eight weeks out), Oklahoma (17 percent of its agency ask funded), Arizona (a $1.45 billion three-year tax cut ahead of an eroding revenue base) and North Carolina (a legislature on record preferring cost reductions to new funding). Those are the four to watch on payment terms, regardless of who wins in November.

**One federal protection survives and is worth invoking.** At redesignated 45 CFR 98.45(m)(4), lead agencies must still ensure providers receive payment under a written payment agreement specifying rates, payment schedules, any fees charged to providers, and a dispute resolution process. Where a state moves to attendance-based or arrears payment, that agreement is the contractual anchor and the dispute-resolution clause is the mechanism.

## 4.2 The rate-staleness gap is the strongest available argument, and it is fully sourced

Six of eleven states are setting reimbursement against benchmarks that predate a period of substantial wage inflation — Alabama by nine years, Kentucky by five, Arizona by four, Virginia by nearly four, Oklahoma by three and a half, North Carolina by three.

In four of those states the evidentiary predicate for an increase already exists, in a state-commissioned or state-published document:

- **Arizona** published a 2024 market rate survey and continues to pay on the 2022 one.
- **Alabama's** own 2024 cost study, conducted by the Department of Human Resources with Alabama State University, documents an average $24-per-week-per-child shortfall and concludes "almost all child care rates were higher than the reimbursement rate."
- **Virginia** enacted a statutory biennial re-benchmarking mechanism in 2026 that has not yet produced numbers, against re-benchmarked cost-per-slot assumptions the state itself published.
- **North Carolina's** own division director told legislators the current funding model wrongly assumes parents can afford quality care and that rates cover less than half the true cost.

The paired comparison that carries furthest is wage floor against public rate. **Florida is the cleanest: the state minimum wage rises 25 percent between September 2022 and September 2026 while the VPK base student allocation moves from $3,029 to $3,029.** Arizona and Virginia produce the same shape with different numbers. In the eight federal-floor states the argument runs on market wages rather than statutory ones, but the arithmetic is the same.

**Two methodological shifts are worth more than any single rate increase**, because they change how rates are set rather than what they are this year: the cost-estimation provision in the federal Child Care Modernization Act, and the state-level equivalents already in motion — Virginia's enacted re-benchmarking, Kentucky's HB 6 mandate to request ACF approval for a cost model for the FFY2028–2030 plan, and Georgia's Georgia Policy Labs cost survey now in the field. **Participation in the Georgia cost survey is a concrete, time-limited action item: cost-based methodologies systematically produce higher benchmarks than price surveys, and the sample is being assembled now.**

## 4.3 Mixed-delivery pre-K is where the money is, and it is unevenly harvested

Across the eleven states, publicly funded pre-K delivered through private providers is the largest identifiable revenue opportunity, and the portfolio's exposure to it is highly uneven.

Where the position is already strong: **Georgia**, where roughly half of a $594 million programme runs through private and community providers, and **Florida**, where private providers are 77 percent of the VPK delivery system.

Where the position is weak relative to the opportunity:

- **Alabama.** Grants of $84,804 to $97,908 per classroom plus up to $125,000 setup, in a programme that has met ten of ten national quality benchmarks for twenty consecutive years, with 4,100 children waitlisted across twenty priority counties and 229 classrooms needed. The stated barriers — classroom space, transportation, wraparound care, workforce — describe exactly what an established multi-site operator already has. **The number required to size this is not published and is obtainable by telephone.**
- **Virginia.** The Mixed Delivery Grant Program is explicitly reserved for licensed non-faith-based private settings, uses a cost-of-quality payment model, contracts rather than vouchers, is re-benchmarked at $19,441 a slot against $6,763 for the school-based programme, and runs at roughly twice oversubscription with a 1,423-family waitlist. **Seven centers is a small position in the state with the best-priced public revenue stream in the portfolio.**
- **Kentucky.** Regulation expressly authorises district contracts with private preschools; a secular for-profit operator clears the sectarian tests trivially; Kentucky serves 26 percent of four-year-olds and lags every mixed-delivery neighbour.
- **Oklahoma.** The unusual case — district pre-K enrollment is *falling*, with the national research body attributing part of the decline to children remaining in child care centers. Roughly 3,500 four-year-olds a year have left the district pipeline since 2019.

Two counterweights belong in any pursuit model. **First, the cost structure.** Every mixed-delivery programme imposes tighter ratios and higher credentials on the funded classroom than the licensing baseline — Alabama at 1:10 with a bachelor's-degree lead teacher against 1:18 licensing; Georgia at 1:10 with K-12 salary parity against 1:18; Arkansas at 1:10 with one degree-holder per three classrooms against 1:15. The result is a two-tier internal labor market inside the same building, and it is the single most common reason mixed-delivery pro formas underperform. **Second, Arkansas demonstrates that the arithmetic can simply fail:** $5,105 per child per year with a 40 percent match obligation and a 15 percent administrative cap is thin-to-loss-leading absent a strong in-kind match strategy.

## 4.4 Quality ratings have decoupled from money in three states, and the decision that follows is deliberate

Quality rating systems were designed to link investment in quality to revenue. In three states in the footprint that link has been cut or is being cut, and the strategic response is not obvious.

- **Tennessee cut it.** The January 2026 restructuring raised base rates ten percent and cut the quality bonus from 15/20 percent to 5/10 percent, producing a net 0.8 percent increase for a top-scoring provider and a full ten percent for a provider with no rating at all.
- **Arkansas collapsed it.** Levels 3–4 now pay identically, as do 5–6, leaving only two rate-relevant advancement steps out of four.
- **North Carolina is studying it.** SL 2025-36 directed a plan to decouple star ratings from reimbursement entirely, due May 1, 2026, in the largest state position in the portfolio.

Where the link remains steep, it is very steep: **Oklahoma's two-star to three-star step raises the infant daily rate 67 percent**, and **Arizona's 40 percent quality differential is worth $25.66 a day per subsidised infant.**

**The correct posture is now state-specific rather than portfolio-wide**, and in several states the business case for a top rating has shifted from subsidy revenue to market access. In Tennessee, three-star status is the hard prerequisite for Voluntary Pre-K contracting across 138 districts; the subsidy bonus no longer justifies it. In Kentucky, All STARS participation is the gate on ECCAP eligibility rather than a meaningful rate premium. In Alabama, Quality STARS gates both state tax credits, which are worth more than the subsidy tiering. In Texas, Texas Rising Star gates the property tax exemption as well as the rate enhancement.

**Two efficiency opportunities are worth testing.** North Carolina's Pathway 3 recognises accreditation and Head Start status, potentially allowing star maintenance on existing national accreditation without the full environment rating scale burden. Oklahoma exempts accredited programmes from partial reviews absent serious non-compliance. In both cases, accreditation already held may be substitutable for assessment burden.

## 4.5 The employer channel is the one clearly expanding revenue line

Everything else in this report describes a public funding environment that is flat, contracting or contested. The employer-partnership channel is the exception, and the reason is federal tax law rather than appropriations.

The 2025 reconciliation law raised the Section 45F credit from 25 percent to **40 percent generally and 50 percent for eligible small businesses**, raised the annual cap from $150,000 to **$500,000 and $600,000**, and — structurally most important — **for the first time permits a qualified facility to be jointly owned or operated by the taxpayer and other persons.** Before 2026, a shared near-site center serving a consortium of mid-size employers in one office park or hospital campus did not qualify. It now does.

Five states in the footprint layer state programmes on top:

| State | Programme | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Georgia** | §48-7-40.6 (75% of operating cost; 100% of property over 10 years) **plus** HB 136's per-child credit | Strongest stack in the portfolio; §48-7-40.6 is the consortium play, HB 136 the tuition-benefit play |
| **Alabama** | Employer credit at 100%/75% of eligible expenses, $600,000 per employer, statewide cap rising to $20M in 2027 | Requires DHR licensure **and** Quality STARS participation |
| **Kentucky** | ECCAP — a **cash matching grant paid directly to the provider**, not a credit | Better for cash flow; stacks with §45F; state match not taxable to the employee |
| **Virginia** | Employee Child Care Assistance Program, enacted May 2026, $25M pilot | Small-business priority; **provider-side participation mechanics [unconfirmed]** |
| **Florida** | Multi-tax credit including **employer payments to a facility on behalf of an employee** | First-come, first-served; **annual cap [unconfirmed]** |

**Three practical notes.** Treasury and IRS guidance on the §45F pooled-facility allocation methodology has not issued, so consortium structures should not be papered without tax counsel. Several state programmes are first-come, first-served against small pools — Alabama's facility credit runs against a $5 million statewide cap with a January reservation window, and Kentucky's ECCAP against $2 million with a 25 percent small-business set-aside — so **timing is as important as eligibility.** And in two states the channel is specifically insulated from what is going wrong elsewhere: **Arizona, where a B2B employer channel sidesteps a 13,500-child subsidy waitlist entirely**, and Kentucky, where ECCAP is a middle-income programme sitting above the subsidy population.

## 4.6 Zoning and siting is the unaddressed policy space — and three states have already opened the door

Across all eleven states, child care siting is essentially a local matter, and in most of them no state-level preemption or by-right provision exists. That is a live constraint on any development pipeline, and it is the area where state-level policy is least developed.

But the door has opened in three states in the last twelve months, in each case through a document already on the public record:

- **Tennessee's Public Chapter 980 amends Title 13, Chapter 7 — local planning and zoning — and carries the state advisory commission's recommendations to align child care zoning with schools and churches, eliminate special appeals for centers in appropriate zones, and cap outdoor play area requirements at state standards.** The operative text has not been obtained.
- **Virginia's Commission on Early Childhood Care and Education recommended, on the record, reducing "bureaucratic barriers to provider entry and expansion, including zoning and licensing simplifications."** That is an existing state endorsement to cite rather than an argument to make.
- **Arkansas's United WE / University of Arkansas report recommends modernising zoning to permit child care in residential areas and establishing "Child Care Zones."** It is foundation-backed, bipartisan-framed and ready for a 2027 sponsor.

North Carolina's Licensed Childcare Licensure Workgroup on conflicts among building, fire and sanitation codes, and Kentucky's HJR 50 Auditor review — which explicitly covers statutes, regulations and agency policies affecting the establishment and operation of child care — are the two other open vehicles.

**Georgia is the notable absence.** No state-level zoning provision was identified in either the 2025 or 2026 sessions, in the state with the second-largest position in the portfolio. SB 554's capital-grant framing is the natural vehicle to attach one to in 2027.

# Appendix — Confidence, Gaps and Method

## A.1 Method

Research was conducted on August 3, 2026 using live web search and direct document retrieval. Primary and authoritative sources were preferred throughout: state administrative codes and licensing rulebooks; legislative bill records and enacted session laws; enacted appropriations acts; agency policy manuals, rate schedules and CCDF state plans; the Federal Register and govinfo bill status data; statutory reports to legislatures; and the National Institute for Early Education Research state profiles. Secondary corroboration came from state policy institutes, advocacy organisation trackers and state-capitol news outlets, and is identified as such in the text.

Nothing time-sensitive in this report is drawn from general knowledge.

## A.2 How to read the confidence marks

**High confidence** items are verified against a primary or authoritative source with a date. These include: the 2026 CCDF final rule and all four rescissions, with citation and effective date; FY2026 enacted federal appropriations; the 2025 federal tax changes to §45F, §21 and §129; every state's licensing ratio table; the 2025 and 2026 legislative outcomes in each state, with bill numbers, sponsors, vote margins and effective dates; enacted state appropriations figures; published reimbursement rate tables and their effective dates; quality rating structures and rate differentials; the 2026 primary and runoff results; and named agency and legislative leadership where verified on an official page.

**Items marked [unconfirmed]** could not be verified from an accessible source during research. They are marked rather than estimated because a flagged gap supports better decisions than a confident guess. **Every such item in this report is closable** — most by a records request, a provider-portal login, or a single telephone call.

## A.4 Independent verification pass

Twelve of the report's highest-stakes claims were re-verified against primary sources on a separate research pass, with the explicit brief of trying to falsify them. The following were confirmed exactly: the 2026 CCDF final rule's citation, type, publication date, effective date and RIN; the scope of all four rescissions and the retained payment-agreement requirement at 45 CFR 98.45(m)(4); the Head Start document's status as a proposed rule and the absence of any final rule affecting 45 CFR 1302 published on or after May 13, 2026; FY2026 enacted CCDBG and Head Start levels against the Administration for Children and Families' own enacted-column table, and Public Law 119-75 dated February 3, 2026; the Section 45F amendments against IRS guidance; the CACFP rate notice; the four brand locator counts used in this report; and every element of Alabama's Childcare Facility Tax Credit.

The pass produced one date correction and three sourcing caveats, all of which have been incorporated:

- **Oklahoma HB 4298 was approved by the Governor on May 12, 2026, not May 28**, and carries an emergency clause added in Senate committee on April 20 — which in Oklahoma makes an act effective on approval rather than the following July 1. The profile now reflects this and flags the effective date for confirmation against the enrolled text.
- **North Carolina's rate-floor provision, its October 1 effective date and the $97 million figure** rest on contemporaneous reporting rather than retrieved session-law text; the enacted act's health and human services special provisions could not be reached. Confirm against the Fiscal Research Division money report.
- **Florida's VPK allocation figures** were read from the enrolled text of the extended-session appropriations bill; the regular-session act died in conference, so the chapter number and approval date should be confirmed alongside the line items.
- **Section 45F wording** now follows the Internal Revenue Service's own formulation — a qualified facility may be "jointly owned or operated by the taxpayer and other persons" — and notes that the caps are inflation-indexed rather than flat.

One apparent contradiction resolved in the report's favour. A bill-tracking service captions Georgia HB 136 (2025) as a foster-child-support-organisation credit bill, and the published Georgia Code still shows the dependent care credit at 30 percent. Both are artefacts: HB 136 passed **by substitute** in both chambers, and the available code snapshot predates the amendment. The Lieutenant Governor's own press release on the signing confirms the 50 percent match, the $250-per-child credit and the $1,000/$500 employer credit as described in this report.

## A.5 Three corrections to circulate internally

Three claims in wide circulation are inaccurate and should not be relied upon.

**1. There is no Arkansas "Act 1435" employer child care tax credit.** A commercial vendor site markets one with a $15 million annual cap, a 30 percent credit rate and a $500,000 per-employer cap, described as active for the 2026 programme year. **Arkansas HB1435 died in House committee at sine die on May 5, 2025 and was never assigned an act number.** The same page attributes oversight to an agency that has not held the portfolio since 2023.

**2. There is no Tennessee employer child care tax credit.** Third-party sites claim a "HB 1163" credit at 50 percent of employer contributions capped at $1 million per employer. **The Tennessee Department of Revenue's published franchise and excise credit list contains no child care credit**, listing only the Job Tax Credit and the Qualified Production Tax Credit.

**3. Florida child care licensing did not move to the Department of Education.** Licensing has remained with the Department of Children and Families throughout the 2021–23 restructuring, which moved only the Office of Early Learning. **Four counties — Broward, Palm Beach, Pinellas and Sarasota — operate their own local licensing agencies.**

Two further corrections to common planning assumptions: **the Alabama Legislature convenes in March 2027, not January**, under the four-year-term rotation; and **Virginia holds no House of Delegates elections in November 2026** — Virginia holds them in odd years, and the next is November 2027.
